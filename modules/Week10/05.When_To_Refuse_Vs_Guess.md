# When to Refuse vs When to Guess

---

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this topic, you should be able to:

- Explain the difference between refusing and guessing in a RAG chatbot
- Describe the four situations where a chatbot should always refuse
- Describe the three situations where a partial answer is better than a refusal
- Configure the refusal boundary in Pinecone Assistant instructions
- Test whether the boundary is set correctly using your golden dataset

---

## Overview

The previous four topics covered how RAG hallucinates, the common failure modes, how to reproduce and fix them, and how to evaluate the pipeline systematically. The chatbot can now answer questions correctly and cite its sources.

But one question has not been fully answered: what should the chatbot do when it is not sure?

A student asks about backpropagation. The uploaded notes cover gradient descent but not backpropagation specifically. The chatbot has two options:

**Option A — Refuse:** "The provided notes do not cover backpropagation in detail."

**Option B — Guess:** "Backpropagation is the algorithm used to train neural networks by propagating gradients backward through the layers, using the chain rule to compute parameter updates."

Option B sounds more helpful. But if the student acts on this answer and the exam uses different terminology or a different explanation from their specific course — the chatbot has actively misled them.

This is the refuse vs guess decision. Getting it right is what makes the chatbot trustworthy rather than just fluent.

---

## Why This Decision Matters

The refuse vs guess decision is not about being conservative or unhelpful. It is about knowing what the chatbot is actually for.

The exam prep chatbot exists to help students revise from their own course material — not to answer general knowledge questions about AI. When it answers from general knowledge instead of the notes, it stops being an exam prep tool and becomes a general chatbot. A general chatbot might give the right answer — but it might also give an answer that conflicts with how the topic is taught in this specific course.

**The real cost of guessing wrong:**
A student revising the night before an exam has limited time. If they spend 20 minutes studying a concept from a chatbot's general knowledge answer — and that answer uses different terminology, a different formula, or a different example from what appears on the exam — those 20 minutes are wasted at best and actively harmful at worst.

**The cost of refusing incorrectly:**
If the chatbot refuses every question even when the notes contain the answer, students stop trusting it. A chatbot that refuses when it should answer is useless.

The goal is to set the boundary precisely — refuse when the notes do not cover the topic, answer when they do.

## The Four Situations Where the Chatbot Should Always Refuse

### Situation 1 — Topic Completely Absent from the Notes

The notes cover Python basics. A student asks about machine learning. No amount of semantic searching will find relevant content — the topic simply is not there.

**Correct response:**
```text
"The provided notes do not cover machine learning. The notes currently
available cover Python fundamentals including variables, loops,
functions, and file handling."
```

The refusal tells the student what the notes do not cover — and what they do cover. This is more useful than a bare refusal because it helps the student understand the scope of the chatbot.

Here is what this correct refusal looks like in Pinecone — the chatbot does not guess about gradient descent, explains what the notes do not cover, and tells the student what the notes do cover instead:

### Situation 2 — Question Requires Information Not in the Notes

The notes say "gradient descent minimises the loss function.**"A student asks "what learning rate should I use for my neural network?"** The notes never specify a learning rate value.

**Correct response:**
```text
"The provided notes explain what the learning rate does but do not
specify which value to use. For specific values, check your course
exercises or ask your lecturer."
```

The chatbot correctly distinguishes between what the notes say (what the learning rate is) and what the notes do not say (which specific value to use).

### Situation 3 — Question Asks for an Opinion or Recommendation

A student asks **"which algorithm is best for this problem?"** Best is a judgment call — the notes cannot make it for the student, and the chatbot should not either.

**Correct response:**
```text
"The notes describe several algorithms and their trade-offs, but do
not recommend one over another. Based on the notes: [describe the
trade-offs]. The choice depends on your specific requirements."
```

The chatbot shares what the notes say about trade-offs without making the judgment the notes do not make.

### Situation 4 — Student Asks About Something Outside the Scope of the Course

A student asks **"who invented gradient descent?"** This is a historical question. The notes never mention inventors.

**Correct response:**
```text
"The provided notes do not cover the history of gradient descent.
They explain how it works and how it is used in training."
```

Even if the chatbot could answer from general knowledge — it should not. The answer might conflict with what the lecturer has said in class.

## The Three Situations Where Partial Answers Are Better Than Refusals

Refusing is not always the right choice. There are three situations where a partial answer — one that says what the notes do cover — is more useful than a bare refusal.

### Situation 1 — The Notes Cover Part of the Answer

A student asks "explain the difference between batch gradient descent and stochastic gradient descent." The notes cover gradient descent but mention batch vs stochastic only briefly.

**Wrong response (too strict):**
```text
"The provided notes do not cover the difference between batch and
stochastic gradient descent in sufficient detail."
```

**Better response (partial answer):**
```text
"The provided notes cover gradient descent in detail [Source 1].
They briefly mention that stochastic gradient descent processes one
example at a time rather than the full dataset [Source 1], but do
not explain the difference in depth. For a full comparison, check
your course textbook or ask your lecturer."
```

The student learns what the notes do say, gets a pointer for where to find more, and knows the chatbot is not guessing.

### Situation 2 — The Answer Can Be Inferred From What the Notes Say

A student asks **"will a high learning rate always cause the model to fail to converge?"** The notes say "a high learning rate causes overshooting" — which implies yes, but the notes do not say it directly.

**Wrong response (guesses the inference):**
```text
"Yes, a high learning rate will always cause the model to fail to
converge because it overshoots the minimum [Source 1]."
```

**Better response (states what notes say, flags the inference):**
```text
"The notes state that a high learning rate causes the algorithm to
overshoot the minimum [Source 1]. Based on this, a very high learning
rate makes convergence unlikely — but the notes do not explicitly
address whether this is always the case. Your course materials may
cover this in more detail."
```

The student gets the information from the notes plus a clear signal that the chatbot is not inventing an absolute claim.

### Situation 3 — The Student Is Close to the Answer

A student asks a question that the notes almost answer — the information is there but in a different phrasing or section.

In this case, retrieve the closest chunk and present it directly:

```text
"The notes do not answer this question directly, but the closest
relevant section says:

[Source 1]: 'The learning rate controls the size of each parameter
update step during gradient descent.'

This may address what you are looking for. If not, the full notes
are available to browse."
```

## Configuring the Boundary in Pinecone Assistant

The refuse vs guess boundary is set in the **Assistant instructions** box. The instructions you set control exactly how Claude behaves when the notes do not contain the answer.

### The Default Instructions (from the RAG Fundamentals Module)

```text
You are an exam preparation assistant for a BTech AI programme.
Answer the student's question using only the lecture notes provided.
After each factual claim cite the source in square brackets — for example [Source 1].
If the notes do not contain enough information to answer the question,
say so clearly rather than guessing.
```

These instructions set a basic boundary — but "say so clearly" is vague. It does not tell Claude what to say, whether to show partial information, or how to handle edge cases.

### Strengthened Instructions for a Precise Boundary

Replace the default instructions with this more precise version:

```text
You are an exam preparation assistant for a BTech AI programme.
Answer the student's question using only the lecture notes provided.
After each factual claim cite the source in square brackets — for example [Source 1].

When the notes do not contain the answer:
- If the topic is completely absent: say "This topic is not covered
  in the provided notes." Then list what topics the notes do cover.
- If the notes cover the topic partially: share what the notes do
  say with citations, then note what is missing.
- If the question asks for a specific value or recommendation the
  notes do not provide: say what the notes say about the concept,
  then note that the specific value or recommendation is not in the notes.
- Never answer from general knowledge. Never use phrases like
  "typically", "generally", or "in most cases" — these signal that
  you are drawing on general knowledge rather than the notes.
```

### Testing the Boundary After Updating Instructions

After updating the Assistant instructions, run your four out-of-scope questions from the golden dataset:

1. Ask a question completely outside the notes — verify it refuses and lists what the notes cover
2. Ask a question the notes partially cover — verify it shares the partial information with a citation
3. Ask a question requiring a specific value the notes do not provide — verify it shares what the notes say without inventing a value
4. Ask a question the notes fully answer — verify it still answers correctly and does not refuse when it should not

If all four behave correctly — the boundary is set. If any produce the wrong behaviour — adjust the specific instruction for that case.

## Best Practices

- Always include explicit refusal instructions in the Assistant instructions — "say so clearly" is not specific enough. Say exactly what to say and when to say it
- Test the boundary with all four question types from the golden dataset after every change to the instructions
- The refusal message should always tell the student what the notes do cover — a bare refusal with no further information leaves the student with nothing
- Never use "typically", "generally", or "in most cases" in the system prompt — these phrases in the instructions signal to Claude that generalising from training is acceptable
- Keep the instructions updated when new files are uploaded — if the knowledge base expands, the refusal message about what topics are covered should be updated too

## Common Beginner Mistakes

- **Setting the refusal too broadly** — instructions that say "only answer if you are 100% certain" cause the chatbot to refuse questions the notes do clearly answer. The boundary should refuse when the notes are absent, not when the chatbot has any uncertainty
- **Setting the refusal too narrowly** — instructions that say "always try to help" allow the chatbot to answer from general knowledge whenever it cannot find a note. This eliminates the grounding that makes RAG useful
- **Not testing after changing instructions** — the refusal boundary must be tested after every change. An instruction that seemed clear can produce unexpected behaviour in practice
- **Forgetting to list what the notes do cover** — a refusal that only says "I cannot answer this" is less useful than one that tells the student what the chatbot can help with

---

## Key Takeaways

- The refuse vs guess decision is about keeping the chatbot grounded in the course material — not about being conservative
- The four situations requiring refusal: topic absent, specific value not in notes, opinion or recommendation requested, outside course scope
- The three situations for partial answers: notes cover part of the answer, answer can be inferred from notes, student is close to the right question
- The refusal message should always say what the notes do not cover and what they do cover
- Configure the boundary in Pinecone's Assistant instructions with specific instructions for each case — not a single vague instruction
- Test the boundary with out-of-scope questions and partial-coverage questions from the golden dataset after every change

> **Interview tip:** If asked "how would you handle questions a RAG chatbot cannot answer?" — describe the distinction between refusing and guessing, name the four situations requiring refusal, and explain that the refusal message should always tell the user what the system does cover. Most candidates say "the chatbot should say it doesn't know." Describing the four specific situations and the partial answer approach for edge cases shows you have thought carefully about the user experience of refusal, not just the technical mechanism.
