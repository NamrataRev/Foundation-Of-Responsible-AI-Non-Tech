# Why "Looks Good" Isn't Evaluation

---

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this topic, you should be able to:

- Explain why human intuition is not a reliable evaluation method for AI systems
- Describe the specific ways "looks good" judgments fail in production
- Identify the difference between casual testing and structured evaluation
- Explain what structured evaluation requires that informal review cannot provide
- Recognise the conditions under which "looks good" is most dangerous

---

## Overview

Think about the last time you read a product description on an online shopping platform. A good one tells you exactly what the product does, why it is better than alternatives, and makes you want to buy it. A bad one is vague, sounds like every other product, or gets a technical detail wrong.

Now imagine a company has built an AI tool that writes product descriptions automatically. A marketing manager wants to check if it is working before rolling it out. She picks ten products, reads the AI-generated descriptions for each one, and thinks — "these sound professional, the details look right, the tone is good." She approves the system.

Three weeks later, problems start appearing. A wireless speaker is described as having "Bluetooth 4.0" when the product actually has Bluetooth 5.2 — a significant difference customers care about. A laptop description calls it "perfect for gaming" when the product has integrated graphics that cannot handle modern games. Two completely different products — a budget option and a premium option — have descriptions so similar that customers cannot tell them apart.

Customers are reading these descriptions and making purchase decisions based on them. Some are buying the wrong product for their needs and returning it. Others are leaving negative reviews saying the product "was not as described."

What went wrong? The manager read ten descriptions and they sounded professional. But sounding professional is not the same as being accurate. She was judging the descriptions the way a customer might skim them — they looked like what product descriptions are supposed to look like. She never checked whether the technical specifications were correct, whether the tone matched the brand, or whether a customer acting on the description would be happy with what they received.

That gap — between "sounds like it is working" and "actually working" — is what this topic is about.

## What "Looks Good" Actually Means

When someone says an AI output "looks good," they are making a judgment based on:

- **Surface fluency** — the text is grammatically correct and reads smoothly
- **Plausible content** — the description sounds like the kind of thing a copywriter might write
- **Familiarity** — the format and tone match what product descriptions are supposed to look like
- **Absence of obvious errors** — nothing immediately jumps out as wrong

None of these four things tell you whether the AI is actually doing its job correctly.

**Think of it like judging a doctor by their bedside manner.** A doctor who speaks confidently, uses appropriate medical vocabulary, and seems warm and attentive will feel trustworthy to most patients — regardless of whether their diagnosis is correct. "Looks good" is bedside manner. What you actually need to evaluate is the diagnosis.

For the product description tool, "looks good" means the description sounds like marketing copy. What you actually need to evaluate is whether the description is:
- **Factually accurate** — are the specifications correct?
- **Brand-consistent** — does it match the company's tone and positioning?
- **Persuasive** — does it give a customer a clear reason to buy?
- **Differentiating** — does it distinguish this product from similar ones?

A description that reads *"Experience powerful wireless audio with advanced connectivity and premium sound quality"* sounds professional. But if the speaker has mediocre sound quality and uses an outdated Bluetooth standard, this description is wrong. It looked good. It was not good.

## Why "Looks Good" Fails — Four Specific Ways

### 1. Fluency Masks Inaccuracy

AI systems are trained to produce fluent, well-structured text. This means they can generate incorrect content in a format that looks completely correct. The product description tool can write grammatically perfect, marketing-toned copy that gets the specifications wrong.

**In the product description tool:** the AI writes *"This laptop delivers outstanding gaming performance with its dedicated graphics and fast processor."* The laptop has integrated graphics — there is no dedicated GPU. The description is factually wrong — but it reads like something a copywriter would write.

Without checking the specification sheet, this error is invisible to a casual reviewer.

### 2. Small Sample Size Hides Failure Patterns

Ten examples is not enough to see patterns. If the AI fails on 15% of products — a significant real-world failure rate — a ten-sample review has a reasonable chance of showing zero failures just by chance. The problem exists. The sample size was too small to surface it.

**In the product description tool:** the AI handles simple consumer electronics well — phones, headphones, cables. It struggles with technical products like routers and graphics cards, luxury items where tone matters most, and budget products where differentiation is hardest. A ten-sample review drawn mostly from simple consumer electronics will miss all three failure categories completely.

### 3. Reviewer Bias Toward Approval (Automation Bias)

When a reviewer reads AI output looking for obvious errors, they tend to find the output more convincing than it is — especially if they already believe the system is working. This is known as **Automation Bias** or confirmation bias. The reviewer is unconsciously applying a lower bar than they would apply to the same content in a higher-stakes context.

**In the product description tool:** the marketing manager reads the AI descriptions knowing she is evaluating the system. She is looking for whether they sound plausible. She is not asking: would a customer who reads this and buys the product be satisfied? Those are different questions, and the second one is much harder to answer on a casual read.

### 4. No Baseline for Comparison

"Looks good" has no reference point. Good compared to what? Without a defined standard — what a correct, high-quality description for this specific product actually looks like — the reviewer has nothing to compare against. They are judging quality in a vacuum.

**In the product description tool:** the manager has a general sense of what product descriptions look like. But she has no written definition of what *correct, high-quality* copy for these specific products looks like. Two reviewers reading the same ten descriptions might reach completely different conclusions — and both would say the descriptions "looked good."

## What "Looks Good" Misses That Structured Evaluation Catches

| What informal review checks | What structured evaluation checks |
|---|---|
| Does it sound professional? | Are the specifications factually correct? |
| Does it look like marketing copy? | Does it meet defined quality criteria consistently? |
| Are there any obvious errors? | What is the error rate across a representative sample? |
| Would I be comfortable publishing this? | Would a customer acting on this description be satisfied? |
| Does it seem reasonable? | Does it produce the same quality for simple and complex products? |

The right column requires a golden dataset, a rubric, and a defined evaluation process. The left column requires reading a few examples and deciding. Both feel like evaluation. Only one is.

---

## The Conditions Where "Looks Good" Is Most Dangerous

Not all informal review carries the same risk. "Looks good" is most dangerous when:

**The stakes are high for the individual receiving the output.**
A customer buying the wrong product based on an inaccurate description wastes money and loses trust in the brand. At scale, this means returns, negative reviews, and long-term brand damage. The higher the impact on the person receiving the output, the more dangerous it is to rely on surface-level approval.

**The failure mode is subtle rather than obvious.**
If the AI described a laptop as "a household cleaning appliance," informal review would catch it. The dangerous failures are the ones that look correct — wrong Bluetooth version, misleading performance claim, generic copy that sounds fine but says nothing. These are precisely the failures that "looks good" cannot detect.

**The volume is high.**
The product description tool will process thousands of products. An informal review of ten samples gives no reliable signal about what happens at scale. A 5% failure rate on ten samples is statistically invisible — on 10,000 product descriptions it is 500 products with wrong or misleading information live on the platform.

Put that in business terms: if 10% of customers who buy a wrongly described product return it, and the average return costs the company $15 in processing, 500 bad descriptions generate $750 in direct costs — before accounting for negative reviews, lost repeat customers, and brand damage. The ten-minute informal review that approved the system cost far more than a proper evaluation would have.

**The reviewer is not the person affected.**
The marketing manager reviewing AI descriptions is not the customer reading them. She is not making a purchase decision based on them. She does not feel the gap between "sounds like marketing copy" and "is actually accurate and useful." The person most qualified to judge the output quality is often not the person doing the evaluation.

## What Real Evaluation Requires

**Why generative AI makes this harder than traditional software:**
Traditional software either works or it does not — you run a test, it passes or fails, the result is the same every time. Generative AI is probabilistic. The same product name and feature list can produce a different description on every run. A description that "looked good" on Tuesday when the manager reviewed it may not be what gets generated on Wednesday when it goes live. Informal review captures one snapshot. Structured evaluation measures the distribution of outputs across many runs — which is the only way to know what customers will actually receive.

**What the difference looks like in practice:**

*Informal review:* the marketing manager opens ten AI descriptions, reads each one for two minutes, notes that they sound professional, and approves the system. Total time: twenty minutes. Evidence produced: none.

*Structured evaluation:* a team of two reviewers independently scores 25 descriptions against a four-dimension rubric, compares scores, resolves disagreements, calculates the pass rate, and compares it against a pre-defined threshold. Total time: three hours. Evidence produced: a documented pass rate, an identified failure category (technical products scoring low on factual accuracy), and a justified deployment decision.

Real evaluation requires four things that informal review cannot provide:

**1. A golden dataset** — a fixed set of product inputs with verified correct descriptions, covering the range of products the system will encounter in production. Not ten convenient examples — a representative sample that includes simple products, technical products, luxury items, budget items, and known difficult categories. This is the subject of the next topic.

**2. A rubric** — a written definition of what quality means for a product description, broken into measurable dimensions with clear scales and anchor examples. Not "does it sound good?" but "does it score 4 or above on factual accuracy, 3 or above on brand tone, and 4 or above on persuasiveness?" This is covered in the rubric design topic.

**3. Consistent scoring** — the same rubric applied to every output by reviewers who agree on what each score level means. This is where inter-rater reliability comes in.

**4. A defined pass/fail threshold** — decided in advance, not after seeing the results.

These four elements together are what turn a subjective impression into a defensible, repeatable evaluation. They are what make it possible to say — with evidence — whether an AI system is ready for production or not.

## The One Question That Replaces "Looks Good"

Every time you feel the urge to approve an AI system because its outputs look good, replace that instinct with one question:

> **"If a customer bought a product based on this description — would they be satisfied with what they received?"**

That question cannot be answered by reading a few samples and nodding. It requires checking the descriptions against the actual product specifications, testing them against the rubric, and measuring the failure rate across a representative sample.

That process — building the evidence to answer this question reliably — is what the rest of this unit teaches you to do.

---

## Best Practices

- Never approve an AI system for production based on fewer than 20-25 evaluated examples — and those examples must be representative, not cherry-picked
- Always test the system on the type of inputs where failure is most consequential — not just the easy, typical cases
- Define what "correct" means in writing before evaluating — not after you have seen the outputs
- Ask someone other than the system builder to evaluate the outputs — the builder is the most likely to see outputs as better than they are

## Common Beginner Mistakes

- **Evaluating on outputs the system was already tested on** — the system will always look good on familiar inputs. Evaluation must use inputs the system has not seen before
- **Confusing fluency with accuracy** — a professionally written wrong product description is more dangerous than a poorly written one, because it is harder to catch
- **Treating the absence of obvious errors as a positive signal** — the absence of obvious errors means only that the obvious errors are absent, not that the subtle ones are
- **Evaluating in the best-case scenario** — if you only test simple products with well-documented specifications, you have no idea how the system behaves on technical or luxury products

---

## Key Takeaways

- "Looks good" is a judgment based on fluency, plausibility, familiarity, and the absence of obvious errors — none of which tell you whether the AI is actually correct
- Fluency masks inaccuracy — AI systems produce professionally written wrong descriptions that casual review cannot detect
- Small sample sizes hide failure patterns — a 5% failure rate is statistically invisible in a ten-sample review and significant at production scale
- The four things real evaluation requires: a golden dataset, a rubric, consistent scoring, and a pre-defined pass/fail threshold
- The conditions where informal review is most dangerous: high individual stakes, subtle failure modes, high volume, and a reviewer who is not the person affected by the output
- Replace "looks good" with one question: would a customer who bought based on this description be satisfied with what they received?

> **Interview tip:** If asked "how did you evaluate your AI system?" — never say "I tested it and it looked good." Describe the golden dataset size, the rubric dimensions, and the pass/fail threshold. Explain what edge cases you included and what failure rate you observed. This shows you understand that evaluation is evidence, not impression.
