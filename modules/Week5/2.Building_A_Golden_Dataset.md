# Building a Golden Dataset

---

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this topic, you should be able to:

- Explain what a golden dataset is and why it is the foundation of structured AI evaluation
- Describe the principles for selecting items that make a golden dataset representative
- Build a golden dataset of 20 or more items for a real AI system
- Identify the categories of inputs that must be included to avoid blind spots
- Explain what makes a verified correct output and how to establish one

---

## Overview

In the previous topic you learned that "looks good" is not evaluation — and that real evaluation requires a golden dataset as its foundation. This topic teaches you how to build one.

The product description tool has been approved for production. The team now needs to evaluate it properly before the next quarterly review. The marketing lead asks: "How do we actually know if the AI is doing a good job across all our products?"

The answer starts here — with a carefully constructed set of reference cases that the system must perform well on before anyone can say with confidence that it is working.

---

## What Is a Golden Dataset?

A **golden dataset** is a fixed collection of inputs paired with verified correct outputs (known in the industry as **Ground Truth**) — a reference set used to measure whether an AI system is producing the quality of output it is supposed to produce.

**Think of it like a standardised exam.** When a university wants to know if students have learned enough to graduate, it does not ask each professor to judge informally. It administers the same exam to every student, scores it against a fixed answer key, and applies a consistent pass mark. The exam is the golden dataset. The answer key is the verified correct output. The pass mark is the threshold.

For the product description tool, the golden dataset is a set of products — with their real specifications — paired with high-quality descriptions that a senior copywriter has verified as correct. When the AI is evaluated, its descriptions are scored against this reference. Not "does this sound good?" — "does this meet the standard the verified description represents?"

---

## Why 20+ Items?

The minimum recommended size for a golden dataset is **20 to 25 items**. Here is the reasoning:

**Statistical reliability:** with fewer than 20 items, a single lucky or unlucky output can swing the pass rate by 5-10 percentage points. At 20+ items, the pass rate becomes a more stable signal — one unusual output changes the rate by at most 5 points.

**Coverage:** a dataset of 10 items can only represent a narrow slice of the inputs the system will encounter. 20+ items allows you to include multiple categories, edge cases, and known difficult scenarios without any single category dominating the sample.

**Credibility:** when you present evaluation results to a stakeholder — a manager, a client, a regulator — a dataset of 10 feels like cherry-picking. A dataset of 25 with documented selection criteria feels like evidence.

**The practical ceiling:** for a course-level evaluation or a new product launch, 20-25 items is the right target. Production systems at scale use hundreds or thousands — but 20-25 is enough to make a defensible deployment decision.

## The Four Categories Every Golden Dataset Must Cover

A golden dataset is only as useful as it is representative. If all 25 items come from the same type of input, you will have high confidence about that one type and no information about anything else.

For the product description tool, every golden dataset must include items from four categories:

### Category 1 — Typical Cases (40-50% of the dataset)

These are the most common inputs the system will encounter in production — the products it was designed to handle well.

**For the product description tool:** mainstream consumer electronics with clear, well-documented specifications. Smartphones, headphones, USB cables, phone cases.

**Why they must be included:** if the system fails on typical cases, nothing else matters. These are the baseline.

**Example items:**
- Sony WH-1000XM5 headphones (well-documented specs, clear use case)
- Samsung 65-inch 4K TV (standard consumer electronics)
- Anker 20,000mAh power bank (simple product, clear features)

### Category 2 — Technical and Complex Cases (20-25% of the dataset)

These are inputs where accuracy is hardest to maintain — products with many technical specifications, industry jargon, or nuanced performance claims.

**For the product description tool:** routers, graphics cards, professional monitors, NAS drives, audio interfaces.

**Why they must be included:** this is where fluency-masking-inaccuracy is most dangerous. A technically wrong description of a router or GPU will mislead customers who specifically care about those specifications.

**Example items:**
- ASUS ROG Strix RTX 4070 graphics card (many technical specs, gaming performance claims)
- Synology DS923+ NAS drive (complex, niche audience)
- Focusrite Scarlett 2i2 audio interface (technical audio specs)

### Category 3 — Edge Cases (20-25% of the dataset)

These are inputs at the boundary of what the system is expected to handle — unusual products, incomplete specifications, or scenarios that test the limits of the system's capability.

**For the product description tool:**
- A product with very few distinguishing features (a plain phone case)
- A product with conflicting information in the specification sheet
- A luxury item where tone matters more than technical detail
- A product with a very long list of specifications that may exceed the context window

**Why they must be included:** real production systems encounter edge cases constantly. A system that passes on typical and technical cases but fails on edge cases will still fail in production — just less predictably.

### Category 4 — Known Failure Cases (10-15% of the dataset)

These are inputs where the system is known to struggle — based on early testing, user feedback, or domain knowledge about the system's weaknesses.

**For the product description tool:** products from categories identified during informal review as problematic — budget items where differentiation is hard, luxury items where the AI defaulted to generic superlatives, or technical products where it hallucinated specifications.

**Why they must be included:** a golden dataset that avoids known weaknesses is not an honest evaluation. Known failure cases must be included to measure whether the system is actually improving on its weak areas over time.

## Building the Dataset — Five Steps

**Step 1 — Define the categories and target count per category**

Write down how many items you need in each category before selecting any. For a 25-item dataset for the product description tool:

```
Typical cases:          10 items (40%)
Technical cases:         6 items (24%)
Edge cases:              6 items (24%)
Known failure cases:     3 items (12%)
Total:                  25 items
```

**Step 2 — Select items within each category**

Choose items that are genuinely representative — not the most convenient, not the ones you already know the system handles well. For each item, note why you selected it and which category it belongs to.

**Step 3 — Gather the verified correct output for each item**

This is the most important and most time-consuming step. For each product, a senior copywriter or domain expert writes or verifies a high-quality description — one that is factually accurate, brand-consistent, persuasive, and differentiating. This is the reference standard the AI will be measured against.

**What makes a verified correct output:**
- It was written or reviewed by someone with domain expertise — not by the AI being evaluated
- It is factually checked against the product specification sheet
- It meets all the quality criteria defined in the rubric (which you will design in the next topic)
- It has been reviewed by at least two people before being accepted as the reference

**Step 4 — Document the dataset**

For each item, record:

| Field | What to record |
|---|---|
| Product name | The exact product being described |
| Category | Typical / Technical / Edge / Known failure |
| Why selected | One sentence explaining why this item is in the dataset |
| Input to the AI | The product name and features as they will be sent to the model |
| Verified correct output | The reference description |
| Key quality criteria | The specific things an evaluator should check for this item |

**Step 5 — Lock the dataset before evaluation begins**

Once the dataset is built and verified, it must not be changed. Adding or removing items after seeing the AI's outputs on them is the evaluation equivalent of moving the goalposts. The dataset is fixed — the AI is evaluated against it, not the other way around.

---

## What Makes a Good Verified Output

The verified correct output is the hardest part to get right — and the most important. Here are the three most common mistakes:

**Mistake 1 — Using the AI's own output as the reference**
If you ask the AI to generate a description, then use that description as the verified correct output, you have built a dataset that measures whether the AI is consistent with itself — not whether it is actually correct. The verified output must come from a source independent of the system being evaluated.

**Mistake 2 — Using one person's opinion**
A single reviewer's judgment is still subject to personal preference and blind spots. The verified output should be reviewed by at least two people who agree it meets all quality criteria.

**Mistake 3 — Verified outputs that are too perfect**
If the verified output is so polished that even a human expert could not produce it consistently, the evaluation bar is unrealistically high. The verified output should represent excellent but achievable quality — the standard a skilled copywriter would meet on a normal working day, not the best output they have ever produced.

## Best Practices

- Set your category counts before selecting any items — it prevents unconscious bias toward easy cases
- Never use the AI being evaluated to generate the verified correct outputs
- Lock the dataset before running any evaluations — no additions or removals after seeing results
- Revisit and update the dataset every six months or whenever the product catalogue changes significantly
- Keep the dataset documentation alongside the prompt version files — they are both part of the same evaluation system

## Common Beginner Mistakes

- **All items from one category** — a dataset of 25 typical cases tells you nothing about how the system handles technical or edge inputs
- **Verified outputs written by the same person who built the system** — the builder is the most likely to rate their own system's outputs as correct
- **Changing the dataset after seeing results** — if the AI scores poorly on three items and you remove them because "they were unfair," you have invalidated the evaluation
- **No documentation of why items were selected** — without this, the dataset cannot be reproduced, audited, or updated consistently

---

## Key Takeaways

- A **golden dataset** is a fixed collection of inputs paired with verified correct outputs — the reference set used to measure AI quality
- The minimum size is **20-25 items** — enough for statistical reliability, category coverage, and credibility
- Every golden dataset must cover four categories: typical cases, technical cases, edge cases, and known failure cases
- The verified correct output must come from a source independent of the AI being evaluated — not from the AI itself
- Lock the dataset before evaluation begins — changing it after seeing results invalidates the evaluation
- Document every item: product, category, why selected, input, verified output, and key criteria to check

> **Interview tip:** If asked "how would you build a test set for your AI system?" — describe the four categories, explain why each is necessary, state the minimum size and the reasoning behind it, and explain how you would establish the verified correct output independently of the system being tested. This shows you understand evaluation as a discipline, not just a step you rush through before deployment.
