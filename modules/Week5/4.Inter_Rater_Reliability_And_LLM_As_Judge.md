# Inter-Rater Reliability and LLM-as-Judge

## Overview

You now have a golden dataset of 25 product descriptions and a rubric with four dimensions, a 1-4 scale, and anchor descriptions for every score level. The next step is to use them — to have reviewers score the AI's outputs and decide whether the system is ready for production.

But here is a problem that most beginners never anticipate: two reviewers, using the same rubric, on the same output, will sometimes give different scores.

Reviewer 1 scores a description 3 on brand tone. Reviewer 2 scores the same description 2 on brand tone. Both read the same anchors. Both are trying to be fair. They still disagree.

When this happens — whose score do you use? And more importantly, what does the disagreement tell you?

This is where **inter-rater reliability** comes in. It measures how consistently different reviewers apply the same rubric — and gives you a way to know whether your evaluation is producing reliable evidence or just varying opinions.

And once your rubric is reliable — once human reviewers agree consistently — you have something even more valuable: a rubric precise enough to be given to an **LLM-as-judge** that can score outputs at scale, automatically, without needing a human to read every single one.

---

## What Is Inter-Rater Reliability?

**Inter-rater reliability** measures the degree to which two or more reviewers independently give the same scores when evaluating the same outputs using the same rubric.

The term sounds technical but it is simple: **inter** means "between," **rater** means "a person giving a score." So inter-rater reliability just means — how much do the two scorers agree with each other?

**Think of it like two referees watching the same football match.** After the match, you ask both of them independently: "Was that a foul in the 67th minute? Yes or no?" If they both say yes — high agreement. If one says yes and the other says no — low agreement. The rules of the game are the rubric. The referees are the reviewers. Their agreement tells you whether the rules are clear enough to produce consistent judgments.

For the product description tool, inter-rater reliability answers: when two people use our rubric to score the same AI-generated description, do they reach the same conclusion? If yes — the rubric is working. If no — something in the rubric is still too vague.

---

## Why It Matters

Low inter-rater reliability means one of two things — and both are problems:

**Problem 1 — The rubric is not clear enough**
If two reviewers consistently disagree on what a "2" looks like for brand tone, the anchors for that dimension need to be more specific. The disagreement is the rubric's fault, not the reviewer's.

**Problem 2 — The task is inherently subjective**
Some dimensions are genuinely hard to agree on — "persuasiveness" has more room for personal interpretation than "factual accuracy." If the disagreement is unavoidable, you need to either narrow the dimension, add more specific anchors, or accept that this dimension requires a senior reviewer to have the final say.

**For the product description tool:** if two reviewers consistently disagree on factual accuracy — a dimension that should be straightforwardly checkable against the specification sheet — the problem is almost certainly a rubric issue. If they occasionally disagree on differentiation — a more subjective dimension — that is expected and manageable.

---

## Measuring Inter-Rater Reliability — Simple Agreement Rate

The simplest measure of inter-rater reliability is the **agreement rate** — the percentage of items where two reviewers gave the same score on the same dimension.

**Step 1 — Both reviewers score the same set of outputs independently**

Do not let them discuss or share scores before they have both finished. Independence is essential — if one reviewer sees the other's score first, they are influenced by it.

**Step 2 — Calculate the agreement rate per dimension**

```
Agreement rate = Number of items where both reviewers gave the same score
                 ÷ Total number of items scored
                 × 100
```

**For the product description tool — example calculation:**

Two reviewers each scored 10 descriptions on the factual accuracy dimension:

| Description | Reviewer 1 | Reviewer 2 | Agree? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sony WH-1000XM5 | 4 | 4 | ✅ |
| ASUS RTX 4070 | 3 | 2 | ❌ |
| Anker power bank | 4 | 4 | ✅ |
| Budget Bluetooth speaker | 2 | 2 | ✅ |
| Focusrite Scarlett | 3 | 3 | ✅ |
| Generic phone case | 4 | 3 | ❌ |
| Samsung 4K TV | 4 | 4 | ✅ |
| Laptop (gaming claim) | 1 | 1 | ✅ |
| Premium speaker | 3 | 3 | ✅ |
| Router | 2 | 3 | ❌ |

```
Agreement rate = 7 agreed out of 10 total = 70%
```

> **Note:** In formal statistics, teams use a metric called **Cohen's Kappa**, which adjusts this agreement rate to account for reviewers who might just be agreeing by lucky chance. For this foundational course, simple agreement rate is sufficient.

**Is 70% good enough?**

| Agreement rate | What it means | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Below 60% | Reviewers are barely agreeing — the rubric is not working | Rewrite the anchors for this dimension before continuing |
| 60–70% | Moderate agreement — some anchors need clarification | Review the disagreements, identify which anchor is ambiguous, revise it |
| 70–80% | Good agreement — rubric is mostly working | Discuss disagreements, align on the ambiguous cases, proceed |
| Above 80% | Strong agreement — rubric is reliable | Proceed with confidence |

For the product description tool, 70% on factual accuracy is at the lower edge of acceptable. The team should review the three disagreements — the ASUS RTX 4070, the generic phone case, and the router — and identify what made the anchor ambiguous for those items.

## Resolving Disagreements — The Calibration Session

When disagreements occur, the fix is a **calibration session** — a structured discussion between the two reviewers about the specific items where they disagreed.

**How a calibration session works:**

**Step 1 — Each reviewer explains their score**
For each disagreement, both reviewers explain what in the output led them to their score. Not "I thought it was a 3" — but "I scored it a 3 because the Bluetooth version was correct but the battery life claim was rounded up by 2 hours, which I read as a minor imprecision."

**Step 2 — Check the anchor together**
Both reviewers look at the anchor for the disputed score level. Does the output match that anchor description? If both reviewers agree on what the anchor says but disagree on whether the output matches it — the anchor needs to be more specific.

**Step 3 — Update the anchor if needed**
If the calibration session reveals that the anchor was genuinely ambiguous, update it. The updated anchor becomes the new reference for all future scoring.

**Step 4 — Re-score the disputed items using the updated anchor**
After the anchor is updated, both reviewers re-score the disputed items independently. If they now agree — the calibration worked.

**For the product description tool — example anchor update:**

Original anchor for Factual Accuracy score 3:
> "All critical specifications are correct. One minor specification is slightly imprecise but not misleading."

The disagreement on the router: one reviewer scored it 3 because the WiFi standard was correct but the range figure was rounded. The other scored it 2 because they felt the range rounding was misleading.

Updated anchor for score 3:
> "All critical specifications are correct. One non-critical specification — such as battery life or listed range — is rounded by no more than 10% of the stated value. The rounding does not affect the customer's purchasing decision."

Now both reviewers have a clear line: rounding by more than 10% drops to a 2. Rounding by 10% or less stays at a 3.

## LLM-as-Judge — Scaling Evaluation Automatically

Once your rubric is reliable — agreement rate above 70% on all dimensions — you have something valuable: a set of anchors precise enough to give to an LLM and ask it to score outputs the same way a human reviewer would.

**LLM-as-judge** is the practice of using an AI model to apply your rubric to AI outputs — automating the evaluation process at scale.

**Think of it like training a new team member to do reviews.** You have a rubric. You have calibrated examples showing what each score looks like. You hand these to a new reviewer and say "use this guide to score these outputs." The new reviewer is not a human — it is an LLM. But if the rubric is clear and the examples are good, the LLM can apply it consistently at a speed no human team can match.

**Why this matters for the product description tool:** the company has 10,000 products. Human reviewers can score 25 items for a golden dataset evaluation. They cannot score 10,000. An LLM-as-judge can.

---

## Designing the LLM-as-Judge Prompt

The LLM-as-judge prompt has four parts. Before you see the full prompt, here is what each part does and why it is there:

**Part 1 — Role and task:** you tell the LLM who it is and what it must do. Just like a system prompt defines a persona, this defines the judge. Without this, the LLM does not know it is supposed to be an evaluator.

**Part 2 — The full rubric:** you paste the complete rubric — all dimensions, the scale, and every anchor description — directly into the prompt. Not a summary. Not a paraphrase. The exact anchors. This is what the LLM uses to make each scoring decision — it is the equivalent of handing the rubric to a human reviewer.

**Part 3 — Few-shot examples:** you show the LLM one or two real examples of a description with its correct scores and a brief explanation of why. This is the calibration — it shows the LLM what "applying the rubric correctly" looks like in practice, not just in theory.

**Part 4 — Output format:** you tell the LLM exactly how to return its scores. JSON is most reliable because your downstream code can read it directly without parsing natural language.

Here is the complete prompt:

```
You are an expert product description evaluator for an e-commerce 
platform. Your task is to score AI-generated product descriptions 
against a four-dimension rubric. You must score each dimension 
independently using only the anchor descriptions provided.

---RUBRIC---

DIMENSION 1: FACTUAL ACCURACY (1-4)
4: Every specification matches the product sheet exactly. No claim 
   is made that cannot be confirmed.
3: All critical specifications are correct. One non-critical 
   specification is rounded by no more than 10%.
2: One critical specification is wrong or misleading — wrong 
   Bluetooth version, incorrect battery life, overstated performance.
1: Two or more critical specifications are wrong, or one claim is 
   so misleading a customer would likely buy the wrong product.

DIMENSION 2: BRAND TONE (1-4)
4: Language consistently matches the brand tier. A reviewer familiar 
   with the brand would not flag any phrase as off-brand.
3: Tone is mostly correct. One or two phrases feel slightly off but 
   the overall impression is right.
2: Tone is noticeably inconsistent. A brand manager would flag this 
   for revision.
1: Tone is completely wrong throughout — reads as if it belongs to a 
   different brand or tier entirely.

DIMENSION 3: PERSUASIVENESS (1-4)
4: At least two specific benefits stated. Value proposition clear in 
   the first two sentences.
3: At least one clear benefit stated. Value proposition present but 
   requires reading to the end to find it.
2: Features listed but not translated into benefits. Customer knows 
   what it has, not why they should want it.
1: No benefit stated. Description is a list of specifications with 
   no explanation of why they matter.

DIMENSION 4: DIFFERENTIATION (1-4)
4: At least one feature or benefit explicitly distinguishes this 
   product from similar alternatives.
3: Some differentiation implied but not stated directly.
2: Description could apply equally to two or three competing 
   products.
1: Swapping the product name with a competitor's would produce a 
   coherent description for that competitor.

---END RUBRIC---

---EXAMPLES---

EXAMPLE 1:
Product: Sony WH-1000XM5 headphones
Specification sheet: 30-hour battery, Bluetooth 5.2, ANC rating: industry-leading
Description: "Experience up to 30 hours of uninterrupted listening 
with Sony's most advanced noise cancellation. Bluetooth 5.2 keeps 
your connection stable across all your devices."

Scores:
- Factual accuracy: 4 — battery and Bluetooth version exact, ANC claim supported
- Brand tone: 4 — measured, confident vocabulary matches Sony premium tier
- Persuasiveness: 3 — benefit of noise cancellation stated, but value proposition 
  requires reading past the first sentence
- Differentiation: 3 — "most advanced noise cancellation" implies superiority 
  but does not compare explicitly to alternatives

EXAMPLE 2:
Product: Budget Bluetooth speaker, $19
Specification sheet: Bluetooth 4.0, 5-hour battery, water resistant IPX4
Description: "Experience premium wireless audio with our 
sophisticated connectivity solution and advanced acoustic engineering."

Scores:
- Factual accuracy: 2 — Bluetooth not mentioned (acceptable) but 
  "advanced acoustic engineering" is a misleading claim for a $19 speaker
- Brand tone: 1 — luxury language completely wrong for a $19 budget product
- Persuasiveness: 1 — no specific benefit stated, only vague marketing language
- Differentiation: 1 — description is entirely generic

---END EXAMPLES---

Now score the following product description. Return ONLY a JSON 
object in this exact format — no other text. You must provide the reason before the score for each dimension to ensure proper chain-of-thought evaluation:

{
  "factual_accuracy_reason": "",
  "factual_accuracy": 0,
  "brand_tone_reason": "",
  "brand_tone": 0,
  "persuasiveness_reason": "",
  "persuasiveness": 0,
  "differentiation_reason": "",
  "differentiation": 0,
  "passes_threshold": true or false
}

Product: [PRODUCT NAME]
Specification sheet: [SPECIFICATIONS]
Description to score: [AI-GENERATED DESCRIPTION]
```

**What this prompt produces:** for every description you send, the LLM returns a JSON object with a score and a one-sentence reason for each dimension, plus a true/false on whether the description passes the threshold. Your code reads the JSON, logs the scores, and flags any description that fails for human review — automatically, for all 10,000 products, in minutes instead of weeks.

## What LLM-as-Judge Does Well — and Where It Falls Short

LLM-as-judge is powerful but not unlimited. Understanding where it works and where it does not prevents over-reliance.

| | LLM-as-judge is reliable | LLM-as-judge is NOT reliable |
|---|---|---|
| **Factual accuracy** | Checking whether a stated specification matches what you provide in the prompt | Checking whether a specification is correct against an external database it cannot access |
| **Format compliance** | Checking whether the description follows a specified structure or length | |
| **Rubric application** | Applying clear, well-anchored rubric criteria consistently at scale | Applying rubric criteria that are inherently subjective or poorly defined |
| **Novel edge cases** | | Scoring outputs that involve product types or contexts significantly different from the examples it was given |
| **Brand tone** | Scoring tone against an explicit written style guide provided in the prompt | Scoring tone based on implicit brand knowledge the LLM may not have |

**The most important limitation:** LLM-as-judge can only check factual accuracy against the information you provide in the prompt. If the product specification sheet is not in the prompt — the LLM cannot verify whether a claim is correct. It will score based on whether the claim sounds plausible, not whether it is true. This is exactly the fluency-masking-inaccuracy problem from the first topic — applied to the judge itself.

**For the product description tool:** always include the full product specification sheet in the LLM-as-judge prompt when scoring factual accuracy. Without it, the judge is evaluating plausibility, not truth.

## When to Use LLM-as-Judge and When to Use Human Review

| Situation | Use LLM-as-judge | Use human review |
|---|---|---|
| Scoring 1,000+ outputs at scale | ✅ | Too slow |
| First evaluation of a new AI system | ❌ | ✅ — establish the baseline with humans |
| Rubric has been calibrated (agreement >70%) | ✅ | |
| Rubric has not yet been calibrated | ❌ | ✅ — calibrate first |
| Factual accuracy check with specs in the prompt | ✅ | |
| Factual accuracy check without specs available | ❌ | ✅ |
| High-stakes output (legal, medical, financial) | ❌ | ✅ always |
| Checking for brand tone against a written style guide | ✅ | |
| Detecting subtle tone issues the LLM might miss | ❌ | ✅ |

**The recommended workflow for the product description tool:**

1. **Calibrate the rubric** — two human reviewers score 25 items, resolve disagreements, achieve >70% agreement
2. **Validate the LLM-as-judge** — run the LLM judge on the same 25 items and compare its scores to the human calibrated scores. If the LLM agrees with humans on >70% of items — it is calibrated
3. **Deploy LLM-as-judge at scale** — use it to score all 10,000 product descriptions
4. **Human spot-check** — every week, a human reviewer scores 20 randomly selected outputs and compares to the LLM judge's scores. If agreement drops below 70% — re-calibrate

## Best Practices

- Always calibrate the rubric with human reviewers before deploying LLM-as-judge — a poorly calibrated rubric produces unreliable LLM scores just as it produces unreliable human scores
- Always include the product specification sheet in the LLM-as-judge prompt when factual accuracy is a dimension
- Use few-shot examples in the judge prompt — at least one passing example and one failing example
- Run weekly human spot-checks on LLM judge scores — the judge can drift as product types change
- Never use LLM-as-judge as the sole evaluation method for high-stakes outputs

## Common Beginner Mistakes

- **Skipping human calibration and going straight to LLM-as-judge** — the LLM cannot calibrate itself. It needs a well-anchored rubric to apply
- **Not including specifications in the factual accuracy prompt** — without the spec sheet, the LLM scores plausibility, not truth
- **Treating LLM judge agreement with itself as a reliability measure** — running the same judge twice and seeing it give consistent scores does not mean it is scoring correctly. You need agreement with calibrated human scores
- **No human spot-checking after deployment** — LLM-as-judge performance can degrade silently as the product catalogue changes

---

## Key Takeaways

- **Inter-rater reliability** measures how consistently different reviewers apply the same rubric — it tells you whether your evaluation is producing reliable evidence or varying opinions
- Calculate agreement rate: number of items where both reviewers agreed ÷ total items × 100
- Agreement below 60% means the rubric anchors need rewriting. 60-70% needs calibration discussion. Above 70% is reliable enough to proceed
- **Calibration sessions** resolve disagreements by having reviewers explain their scores, checking the anchor together, and updating it where needed
- **LLM-as-judge** applies your calibrated rubric automatically at scale — it is only as reliable as the rubric and examples you give it
- Always include source documents (specification sheets) in the judge prompt for factual accuracy checks
- Validate LLM-as-judge against human scores before deploying at scale, and spot-check weekly after deployment
- Never use LLM-as-judge as the sole evaluation method for high-stakes outputs

> **Interview tip:** If asked "how would you scale evaluation of AI outputs beyond what a human team can review?" — describe LLM-as-judge with the full workflow: calibrate the rubric with humans first, validate the LLM judge against those human scores, deploy at scale, spot-check weekly. Most candidates say "use AI to check AI" without explaining how you know the AI judge is reliable. The calibration-first workflow is what shows you understand the limits of the approach.
