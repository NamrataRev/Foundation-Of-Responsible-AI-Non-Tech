# PII, PHI, Copyright, Confidentiality and Model Cards

---

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this topic, you should be able to:

- Define PII and PHI and explain why they require special protection in AI systems
- Explain copyright obligations when using AI — for training data, generated content, and uploaded documents
- Define confidentiality and explain when it applies in an AI system context
- Read and interpret a model card before deploying a pre-trained model
- Apply all four concepts to the exam prep chatbot scenario

---

## Overview

The previous three topics covered the legal frameworks — EU AI Act, NIST AI RMF, and DPDP Act. This topic covers the specific data types and documentation that those frameworks are designed to protect and require.

Every AI system handles data. Not all data is equal. Some data — personal information that identifies individuals — requires protection by law. Some data — health information — requires even stricter protection. Some data — copyrighted material — carries legal obligations about how it can be used. And some information — confidential organisational data — carries contractual and ethical obligations that go beyond what any law specifically requires.

Model cards are the documentation mechanism that connects all of this — they are the document that tells you what a model was trained on, what it is designed to do, what it cannot do, and what risks it carries.

## Part 1 — PII: Personally Identifiable Information

### What PII Is

**Personally Identifiable Information (PII)** is any data that can be used to identify a specific individual — directly or indirectly.

PII is not an Indian legal term — it comes from US law and is widely used in international data governance. Under the DPDP Act, the equivalent concept is "personal data." Under GDPR, it is "personal data." Under US HIPAA and FERPA, it is PII. The term differs by jurisdiction but the concept is the same.

**Direct PII — identifies a person on its own:**
- Full name
- Email address
- Phone number
- National ID number (Aadhaar, passport)
- Student ID number
- Photograph
- Biometric data (fingerprint, face scan)
- IP address (in many jurisdictions)

**Indirect PII — identifies a person in combination:**
- Date of birth + city + gender (combination can narrow to one person)
- Job title + employer + department (can identify one employee)
- Medical condition + treatment location + approximate age

**Why indirect PII matters for AI systems:** AI systems aggregate data. A chatbot that stores question history, session timestamps, device information, and subject preferences may not store any direct PII — but the combination can identify a specific student. This is called the **aggregation problem** and it is a common source of unintended PII exposure in AI systems.

### PII in the Exam Prep Chatbot

```text
DATA THE CHATBOT PROCESSES          PII STATUS
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Student email address               ✅ Direct PII
Student name (if collected)         ✅ Direct PII
Uploaded lecture notes              ⚠️  PII if notes contain
                                    student's own name or
                                    identifying information
Question history (linked to account)⚠️  Indirect PII —
                                    pattern can identify student
Session timestamps                  ⚠️  Indirect PII in combination
API usage logs                      ⚠️  Indirect PII in combination
Anonymous aggregated statistics     ❌  Not PII
```

### What to Do With PII

- **Collect minimum necessary** — if the chatbot does not need the student's full name to function, do not collect it
- **Store securely** — encrypted at rest and in transit
- **Delete when no longer needed** — define retention periods and enforce them
- **Never log PII in plain text** — API logs, error logs, and debugging logs must not contain PII
- **Anonymise for analysis** — if you need to analyse usage patterns, remove or hash identifying information first

## Part 2 — PHI: Protected Health Information

### What PHI Is

**Protected Health Information (PHI)** is a subset of PII specifically relating to health — medical conditions, diagnoses, treatments, prescriptions, mental health records, and any information that connects a health status to an identifiable individual.

PHI is subject to stricter protections than general PII in almost every jurisdiction:

| Jurisdiction | Health data law | Key requirement |
|---|---|---|
| India | DPDP Act (sensitive personal data) | Additional consent requirements for health data |
| EU | GDPR Article 9 | Special category data — explicit consent or specific legal basis required |
| US | HIPAA | Covered entities must implement specific safeguards, breach notification within 60 days |

### PHI in AI Systems

PHI creates specific risks in AI systems that general PII does not:

**Training data risk:** if a model is trained on health records without proper anonymisation, the model may leak PHI in its outputs — a phenomenon called **training data memorisation**. A model asked to complete a sentence about a patient may reproduce actual patient records it was trained on.

**Retrieval risk in RAG systems:** if a RAG chatbot is built on medical records, a question that retrieves the wrong chunk could expose one patient's health information to a different user. This is the RAG retrieval failure — but with PHI, the consequence is a data breach with legal penalties.

**For the exam prep chatbot:** PHI is unlikely to arise unless the chatbot is used by medical students uploading clinical case notes. If case notes contain real patient information, the chatbot is processing PHI and stricter obligations apply — including explicit consent, enhanced security, and breach notification requirements.

**The safe approach:** if students are uploading clinical notes, require that all patient information is anonymised before upload. Build this requirement into the consent notice and the onboarding instructions.

## Part 3 — Copyright

### What Copyright Means for AI Systems

Copyright is automatic. The moment someone creates an original work — a book, an article, a lecture, a piece of code — copyright exists. No registration is required in India, the EU, or most other jurisdictions. The copyright holder has exclusive rights to reproduce, distribute, adapt, and perform the work.

AI systems interact with copyright in three distinct ways — each with different obligations.

### Copyright in Training Data

When an AI model is trained on text, it learns from that text. If the training data includes copyrighted material — books, articles, code — the model has processed copyrighted content. Whether this constitutes copyright infringement is a live legal question being litigated in multiple jurisdictions as of 2026.

**Current position:**
- In the EU, GDPR and the Digital Single Market Directive allow text and data mining for research purposes with opt-out provisions
- In India, there is no specific AI training data exception — the general fair dealing provisions of the Copyright Act 1957 may apply
- Model providers like Anthropic publish copyright compliance summaries as part of their GPAI obligations under the EU AI Act

**For the exam prep chatbot:** Claude was trained by Anthropic. The training data copyright obligations are Anthropic's — not the student deploying the chatbot. However, the student has copyright obligations of their own.

### Copyright in Uploaded Documents

When a student uploads lecture notes to the exam prep chatbot, those notes are processed by the system. Copyright questions arise:

**The lecturer's copyright:** lecture notes created by a lecturer are typically the lecturer's intellectual property (or the university's, depending on the employment contract). A student who attends a lecture and writes their own notes owns those notes. A student who photographs the lecturer's slides or copies the lecturer's notes verbatim does not own copyright in that material.

**What this means for the chatbot:** if a student uploads the lecturer's own slides or a textbook, the upload may infringe copyright. The consent notice should specify that students are only permitted to upload material they own or have permission to use.

**Published textbooks:** uploading a textbook PDF to the chatbot is almost certainly copyright infringement — the student is reproducing and processing a copyrighted work without permission. This is true even if the use is private.

### Copyright in AI-Generated Output

When the exam prep chatbot generates an answer, who owns that answer?

**Current legal position:**
- In India, the Copyright Act 1957 requires human authorship for copyright to exist. AI-generated content with no human creative input is not protected by copyright under Indian law.
- In the EU, the legal position is evolving — some jurisdictions recognise copyright in AI-assisted works where a human made creative choices.
- In the US, the Copyright Office has stated that purely AI-generated content without human authorship is not copyrightable.

**Practical implication:** an answer generated by the exam prep chatbot is not the student's copyrighted work. The student cannot copyright it. The student also cannot claim it was their own work if submitting it as an assignment — that is academic integrity, covered below.

### Academic Integrity and Copyright

Using AI to generate assignment answers is not primarily a copyright issue — it is an academic integrity issue. Most universities have policies prohibiting submission of AI-generated work as the student's own. These policies are separate from copyright law but interact with it.

The exam prep chatbot is designed for revision — asking questions and getting cited answers from uploaded notes. Using it to generate assignment answers goes beyond its intended purpose and likely violates academic integrity policies.

## Part 4 — Confidentiality

### What Confidentiality Means

Confidentiality is an obligation — legal, contractual, or ethical — to keep specific information private. Unlike copyright (which protects creative works) or PII (which protects personal information), confidentiality can apply to any type of information.

**Sources of confidentiality obligations:**

| Source | Example |
|---|---|
| Contract | A non-disclosure agreement (NDA) prohibiting disclosure of a company's trade secrets |
| Professional duty | A lawyer's duty of confidentiality to their client; a doctor's duty to their patient |
| Employment | An employee's obligation not to disclose their employer's confidential business information |
| Law | Attorney-client privilege; doctor-patient privilege |
| Terms of service | Agreeing to keep beta product features confidential |

### Confidentiality in AI Systems

The risk AI systems create for confidentiality is that they make it easy to inadvertently disclose confidential information to a third-party system.

**The confidentiality breach pattern:**
1. Employee has access to confidential company information
2. Employee uploads that information to an AI chatbot to get help with a task
3. The AI chatbot processes the information — it now exists on a third-party server
4. The AI provider's terms of service may allow the information to be used for model training
5. Confidential information has been disclosed to a third party

This is not hypothetical. In 2023, Samsung engineers uploaded proprietary semiconductor code to ChatGPT to help debug it. The code — Samsung's trade secret — was processed by OpenAI's systems. Samsung subsequently banned the use of external AI tools for work tasks.

**For the exam prep chatbot:** if a student uploads notes that include confidential university research, examination questions that have not yet been sat, or personal information about other students — the upload creates a confidentiality breach. The consent notice must prohibit uploading confidential information.

### The Anthropic Privacy Position

Anthropic's current privacy policy states that conversation data submitted through the API (which is how the exam prep chatbot interacts with Claude) is not used for training models by default. Organisations can request data retention policies as part of their API agreement.

**The practical principle:** before uploading any information to an AI system, ask: if this information appeared in a data breach, what would the consequences be? If the answer is "serious" — the information should not be uploaded.

## Part 5 — Model Cards

### What a Model Card Is

A model card is a document that describes an AI model — its intended use, its training data, its performance characteristics, its known limitations, and its potential risks and harms. It is the equivalent of a product safety data sheet for an AI model.

Model cards were first proposed by Google researchers in 2018. They are now required for GPAI model providers under the EU AI Act and are widely adopted as a voluntary practice.

**What a model card typically contains:**

| Section | What it covers |
|---|---|
| **Model details** | Model name, version, type, developers, release date |
| **Intended use** | What the model is designed to do, who it is designed for |
| **Out-of-scope use** | What the model is not designed for — explicitly stated |
| **Training data** | What data was used to train the model, with what characteristics |
| **Performance metrics** | How the model performs on standard benchmarks |
| **Evaluation results** | Results of bias and fairness evaluations |
| **Limitations** | Known failure modes, what the model does not do well |
| **Ethical considerations** | Known risks, potential for misuse, recommended safeguards |
| **Recommendations** | What precautions deployers should take |

### Why Model Cards Matter Before Deployment

Deploying a model without reading its model card is making a consequential decision without available information. The model card tells you:

- Whether the model's intended use matches your use case
- What populations or tasks the model performs poorly on
- What safeguards the model provider recommends
- What data the model was trained on and whether that creates copyright or bias concerns

**Example — reading the model card before deploying the exam prep chatbot:**

Anthropic publishes usage documentation and a responsible scaling policy that together function as a model card for Claude. Key information relevant to the exam prep chatbot:

```text
RELEVANT MODEL CARD INFORMATION FOR THE EXAM PREP CHATBOT

Intended use:
Claude is designed for helpful, harmless, and honest conversations.
It is suitable for question-answering from provided context.
→ Matches exam prep chatbot use case ✅

Limitations relevant to exam prep:
Claude may generate plausible-sounding but incorrect information
when the provided context is insufficient.
→ Matches the hallucination risk already managed ✅

Training data:
Claude was trained on text data from the internet. Training data
copyright summary published as part of GPAI obligations.
→ Students uploading their own notes are not affected ✅

Out-of-scope use:
Claude should not be used to generate content that violates
academic integrity policies or to complete student assignments.
→ Matches the academic integrity limitation already noted ✅
```

### How to Read a Model Card — A Checklist

Before deploying any pre-trained model, work through these questions using the model card:

```text
☐ Does the model's intended use match my use case?
  If no → do not deploy without understanding why and whether
  the mismatch creates risk

☐ Are there known limitations that affect my use case?
  If yes → implement mitigations before deployment
  (the RAG pipeline failure modes covered in Week 10 are one example)

☐ What data was the model trained on?
  If training data included copyrighted material → understand
  the provider's compliance position

☐ Are there known bias or fairness issues?
  If yes → evaluate whether those issues affect your users

☐ What safeguards does the provider recommend?
  Always implement → this is the minimum expected standard

☐ Is there a GPAI documentation summary (EU AI Act requirement)?
  If the model is used by EU users → the provider must have
  published this; retrieve and review it
```

## Best Practices

- Read the model card before deploying any pre-trained model — it tells you what the model was designed for and what it was not
- State upload restrictions clearly in the consent notice — students must be told they cannot upload copyrighted textbooks, confidential information, or unredacted clinical notes
- Apply the minimum necessary principle to PII — collect only what the system needs to function, store it only as long as needed
- Treat the aggregation problem seriously — combinations of non-PII data can create PII. Review what your system stores in combination, not just individually
- Never upload confidential information to a third-party AI system — the Samsung incident is the reminder. If you would not email it to a stranger, do not upload it to a chatbot

## Common Beginner Mistakes

- **Assuming only names and email addresses are PII** — session histories, usage patterns, and uploaded documents can all be PII in combination
- **Uploading copyrighted material to test the system** — even a test upload of a textbook PDF may constitute copyright infringement
- **Assuming AI-generated content is the user's intellectual property** — under current law in India and most jurisdictions, purely AI-generated content has no copyright owner
- **Not reading the model card before deployment** — the model card is available, the information is free, and not reading it means making a deployment decision without available information

## Key Takeaways

- **PII** is any data that identifies an individual directly or in combination — the aggregation problem means combinations of non-PII can become PII
- **PHI** is health-related PII — subject to stricter protections and specific breach notification requirements
- **Copyright** affects AI systems in three ways: training data (the model provider's obligation), uploaded content (the user's obligation), and generated output (currently not copyrightable in most jurisdictions)
- **Confidentiality** can apply to any information by contract, professional duty, or law — uploading confidential information to a third-party AI system is likely a breach
- **Model cards** are the documentation that describes a model's intended use, limitations, training data, and known risks — reading one before deployment is the minimum expected standard
- All four concepts interact: a student uploading their own handwritten notes is unlikely to create PII, PHI, copyright, or confidentiality issues — a student uploading a textbook PDF creates copyright issues; uploading clinical case notes creates PHI issues; uploading exam papers creates confidentiality issues

> **Interview tip:** If asked "what data protection considerations apply when building an AI system?" — name PII and PHI as distinct concepts, explain the aggregation problem, describe copyright obligations for training data and generated output separately, mention confidentiality and the third-party disclosure risk, and name model cards as the documentation mechanism. Most candidates say "we need to protect user data." Naming four distinct data types with different legal frameworks and the model card as the documentation that connects them shows you understand data protection as a multi-dimensional engineering obligation.
