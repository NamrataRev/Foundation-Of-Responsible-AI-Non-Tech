# The DPDP Act — India's Data Protection Law

---

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this topic, you should be able to:

- Explain what the Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023 is and who it applies to
- Define personal data, data fiduciary, data principal, and consent under the Act
- Identify what obligations the Act places on organisations building AI systems in India
- Classify the exam prep chatbot under the DPDP Act
- Explain how the DPDP Act relates to the EU AI Act and GDPR

---

## Overview

The EU AI Act governs what AI systems can do. The NIST AI RMF provides a framework for managing AI risk. The DPDP Act governs something both of the above take for granted: **the data that AI systems are built on and trained with.**

Every AI system processes data. The exam prep chatbot processes student questions, uploaded lecture notes, and session history. If any of that data relates to an identifiable person — a student's name, email address, student ID, or even a pattern of questions that could identify them — it is personal data under the DPDP Act.

India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023 is the country's primary data protection law. It came into force in August 2023 and its rules are being notified in phases. For BTech engineers building AI systems in India — or building systems that process data of Indian citizens — this law applies.

---

## Key Terms

| Term | Definition under DPDP Act |
|---|---|
| **Personal data** | Any data about an identifiable individual — name, email, phone number, student ID, or any combination that can identify a person |
| **Data fiduciary** | An organisation or person who determines the purpose and means of processing personal data — equivalent to "data controller" under GDPR |
| **Data principal** | The individual whose personal data is being processed — the student, in the exam prep chatbot scenario |
| **Consent** | Freely given, specific, informed, and unambiguous agreement by the data principal to their data being processed |
| **Data processor** | An organisation that processes data on behalf of a data fiduciary — a cloud hosting provider, for example |
| **Significant data fiduciary** | A data fiduciary designated by the government as processing large volumes of sensitive data or posing significant risk — subject to additional obligations |
| **Data localisation** | The requirement to store certain data within India's geographic boundaries |

## What Counts as Personal Data

This is the question most engineers get wrong. Personal data is not just a name and phone number. It is any data that can identify a person — directly or in combination with other data.

**Clearly personal data:**
- Student name
- Email address
- Student ID number
- Mobile number
- Photograph

**Personal data in context — the exam prep chatbot:**
- A student's uploaded notes — if they contain the student's name or identifying information
- Session history — if questions can be linked to a specific user account
- Login credentials — email or student ID used to access the chatbot
- Usage patterns — if a specific pattern of questions over time identifies a student

**What is NOT personal data:**
- Anonymised data where re-identification is not possible
- Aggregated statistics — "60% of students asked about while loops" — with no individual identifiable
- The lecture notes themselves — if they do not contain personal information about any individual

**For the exam prep chatbot:** the moment a student logs in with an email address and the system stores their questions and uploaded notes against that account — personal data is being processed. The DPDP Act applies from that moment.

## The Three Core Obligations

### Obligation 1 — Lawful Processing With Valid Consent

Under the DPDP Act, personal data can only be processed for a lawful purpose with the data principal's consent — or under one of the specified legitimate use exceptions.

**What valid consent requires:**
- **Free** — the student must not be forced or manipulated into consenting
- **Specific** — the consent must describe exactly what data is collected and for what purpose
- **Informed** — the student must understand what they are agreeing to
- **Unambiguous** — the consent must be a clear affirmative action, not a pre-ticked box

**For the exam prep chatbot:**
Before a student uploads their notes and asks questions, the system must:

```text
REQUIRED CONSENT NOTICE (example):

"This chatbot stores your uploaded notes and question history
to provide personalised exam preparation assistance. Your data
is processed by [University Name] and stored on [Platform].

Your data will be used for:
✓ Answering your questions using your uploaded notes
✓ Improving the chatbot's responses (anonymised only)

Your data will NOT be used for:
✗ Sharing with third parties
✗ Grading or assessment decisions
✗ Any purpose beyond exam preparation

You can withdraw consent and request deletion of your data
at any time by contacting [contact details].

[ ] I agree to the processing of my personal data as described above."
```

The checkbox is required. A banner the student clicks through is not sufficient consent under DPDP.

---

### Obligation 2 — Data Principal Rights

The DPDP Act gives every data principal — every student using the chatbot — specific rights that the data fiduciary must honour.

| Right | What it means for the exam prep chatbot |
|---|---|
| **Right to information** | Students must be told what data is collected, for what purpose, and for how long it is stored |
| **Right to correction** | Students can request that incorrect personal data about them be corrected |
| **Right to erasure** | Students can request that their personal data be deleted — uploaded notes, question history, account data |
| **Right to grievance redressal** | Students must have a way to raise complaints about how their data is handled |
| **Right to nominate** | Students can nominate someone to exercise their rights on their behalf in case of death or incapacity |

**For the exam prep chatbot:** at minimum, the system must provide:
- A contact point for data-related requests
- A process for deleting a student's uploaded notes and session history on request
- A response to data requests within a reasonable timeframe (specific timelines will be defined in rules)

---

### Obligation 3 — Security Safeguards

Data fiduciaries must implement reasonable security safeguards to prevent personal data breaches.

**What this means in practice for the exam prep chatbot:**
- Student uploaded notes must not be accessible to other students
- Login credentials must be stored securely (hashed, not plain text)
- API keys and access tokens must not be stored in code (the `.env` file principle covered in Week 8 applies here as a legal requirement, not just good practice)
- If a data breach occurs, the Data Protection Board of India must be notified

## Significant Data Fiduciaries — Additional Obligations

The government designates certain organisations as Significant Data Fiduciaries based on the volume and sensitivity of data they process. A large university deploying the exam prep chatbot to thousands of students could potentially be designated as a Significant Data Fiduciary.

**Additional obligations for Significant Data Fiduciaries:**
- Appoint a Data Protection Officer (DPO) based in India
- Conduct periodic Data Protection Impact Assessments (DPIA)
- Conduct periodic audits by an independent data auditor
- Ensure algorithmic accountability — document how automated decisions are made

**For the exam prep chatbot at a small university:** unlikely to trigger Significant Data Fiduciary status for the chatbot alone. But if the university processes large volumes of student data across many systems, the chatbot's data may be considered alongside all other processing.

## Sector-Specific Considerations

The DPDP Act applies across all sectors but certain sectors have additional data protection obligations layered on top.

### Education

Student data — grades, attendance, learning records — is sensitive. While DPDP does not classify student data as a special category requiring extra protection (unlike health data), educational institutions have obligations under UGC guidelines and the Right to Education Act that interact with DPDP.

**For the exam prep chatbot:** if the chatbot is used to inform assessment decisions — for example, if session data is reviewed by a lecturer to understand how a student prepared — that use must be disclosed in the consent notice. Using data for a purpose not disclosed at consent is a DPDP violation.

### Healthcare

Health data — medical records, diagnoses, prescriptions — is sensitive personal data under DPDP. If an AI system processes health data, stricter protections apply. A chatbot helping medical students with clinical notes would process health-related content and potentially health data of patients mentioned in case studies.

### Financial Services

Financial data is regulated by RBI guidelines in addition to DPDP. AI systems processing account data, credit information, or transaction history must comply with both DPDP and sector-specific RBI requirements.

## How DPDP Relates to GDPR and EU AI Act

Students building AI systems for international use will encounter both DPDP and GDPR. They are similar in principle but different in specifics.

| | DPDP Act (India) | GDPR (EU) |
|---|---|---|
| **Applies to** | Personal data of Indian citizens | Personal data of EU residents |
| **Consent standard** | Freely given, specific, informed, unambiguous | Same |
| **Data subject rights** | Information, correction, erasure, grievance, nomination | Information, access, correction, erasure, portability, objection |
| **Breach notification** | To Data Protection Board of India | To supervisory authority within 72 hours |
| **Penalties** | Up to ₹250 crore (approx. €27M) per instance | Up to €20M or 4% of global turnover |
| **Children's data** | Parental consent required under 18 | Parental consent required under 16 (varies by member state) |

**The overlap for the exam prep chatbot:** if the chatbot is used by students in both India and the EU, both DPDP and GDPR apply simultaneously. The consent notice must satisfy both standards. The data rights must cover both sets of rights. Security safeguards must meet both requirements.

**Practical approach:** design to the stricter standard. GDPR's 72-hour breach notification is stricter than DPDP's current requirements. GDPR's right to data portability has no direct equivalent in DPDP. Building to GDPR standards generally satisfies DPDP as well.

## The Exam Prep Chatbot — DPDP Compliance Checklist

```text
BEFORE DEPLOYMENT:
☐ Identify all personal data collected — email, uploaded notes, session history
☐ Draft a consent notice that is specific, informed, and unambiguous
☐ Build a consent mechanism — explicit checkbox, not a banner
☐ Define data retention periods — how long is data stored after a student stops using the chatbot?
☐ Build a deletion process — students must be able to request erasure
☐ Secure API keys and access credentials — never in code, always in environment variables
☐ Document the data flow — who processes what, where it is stored, who has access

AFTER DEPLOYMENT:
☐ Respond to data requests within required timeframes
☐ Notify Data Protection Board in the event of a breach
☐ Review consent notice whenever the system's data practices change
☐ Conduct a Data Protection Impact Assessment if scale increases significantly
```

## Best Practices

- Build consent into the system from the start — retrofitting a compliant consent mechanism is harder than building it correctly the first time
- Store the minimum data necessary — if the chatbot does not need to retain session history, do not retain it
- Never store API keys in code — what Week 8 taught as good engineering practice is a legal requirement under DPDP's security safeguard obligation
- Design data rights as features — the ability for a student to delete their data is a user feature, not just a compliance checkbox
- If building for Indian and EU users simultaneously — design to the stricter standard across both frameworks

## Common Beginner Mistakes

- **Assuming DPDP only applies to large companies** — it applies to any organisation processing personal data of Indian citizens, regardless of size
- **Treating a cookie banner as consent** — DPDP requires specific, informed, unambiguous consent. A banner the user clicks through to access the service is not valid consent
- **Forgetting that uploaded documents may be personal data** — if a student's notes contain their name or identifying information, those notes are personal data under DPDP
- **Not building a deletion process** — the right to erasure is a legal obligation. If there is no deletion mechanism, the chatbot is non-compliant from day one

## Key Takeaways

- The DPDP Act 2023 governs how personal data of Indian citizens is collected, stored, and processed — it applies to any AI system that processes such data
- **Personal data** is any data that identifies an individual directly or in combination — student emails, uploaded notes linked to accounts, and session history all qualify
- The three core obligations are: lawful processing with valid consent, honouring data principal rights, and implementing security safeguards
- **Consent** must be free, specific, informed, and unambiguous — a pre-ticked checkbox or banner click is not sufficient
- Data principals have five rights: information, correction, erasure, grievance redressal, and nomination
- The DPDP Act and GDPR are similar in principle — when building for both Indian and EU users, design to the stricter standard
- Never storing API keys in code — taught as engineering practice in Week 8 — is also a DPDP legal requirement under security safeguards

> **Interview tip:** If asked about data protection in AI systems — name DPDP (for India) and GDPR (for EU), distinguish data fiduciary from data principal, explain the consent standard, and list at least three data principal rights. Then apply it to the system being discussed — identify what personal data it processes and what consent mechanism is required. Most candidates say "we need to protect user data." Naming the specific law, the consent standard, and the data rights shows you understand data protection as a legal obligation with specific requirements.
