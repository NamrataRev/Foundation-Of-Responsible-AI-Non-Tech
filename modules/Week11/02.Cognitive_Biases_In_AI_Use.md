# Cognitive Biases in AI Use

---

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this topic, you should be able to:

- Explain how each of the four cognitive biases shows up specifically when using an AI chatbot
- Identify the availability heuristic and describe how it distorts reliability judgements
- Describe how human biases get encoded into AI systems through training data and labelling
- Recognise which bias is operating in a given human-AI interaction
- Apply one practical countermeasure for each bias when using the exam prep chatbot

## Overview

The Human Cognition and AI Oversight topic introduced the four cognitive biases that affect how people interact with AI: automation bias, anchoring, confirmation bias, and the availability heuristic. This topic goes one level deeper — not redefining the biases, but showing exactly how each one shows up in practice when a student uses the exam prep chatbot, and adding one dimension that the intro topic did not cover: how human biases get encoded into AI systems before any student ever types a question.

The exam prep chatbot is built on documents that humans wrote, data that humans labelled, and systems that humans designed. Every step in that chain is a place where a human bias could enter — and once it is inside the system, it comes out in every answer the chatbot gives.

## How Each Bias Shows Up in Practice

### Automation Bias — Trusting the Format

The intro topic defined automation bias as trusting automated outputs more than human judgement. In practice with the exam prep chatbot, it shows up in one very specific way: **students trust the citation format itself as evidence of verification.**

When a student sees:

```text
A while loop repeats as long as a condition stays True [Source 1].
It is used when you do not know in advance how many times to repeat [Source 1].
```

The `[Source 1]` marker creates an impression that someone has already checked this. The citation looks like a footnote in a textbook. Textbook footnotes are verified. Therefore this must be verified too.

But `[Source 1]` was generated by the same model that generated the answer. It was not checked by a human. Clicking it reveals the chunk — but students experiencing automation bias do not click. The format convinced them the work was already done.

**The countermeasure:** make clicking citations a deliberate habit, not an optional step. Treat every `[Source N]` as a pointer to check, not as proof the claim is correct. The citation is a map reference — it tells you where to look, not that the destination is what the answer claimed.

---

### Anchoring — The First Answer Shapes Everything After

The intro topic defined anchoring as the first piece of information disproportionately influencing all subsequent thinking. In practice with the exam prep chatbot, it shows up when a student asks about a topic early in a revision session and the chatbot's answer becomes the mental frame for every note they read after.

**What this looks like:**

A student asks: *"What is a for loop?"*

The chatbot answers: *"A for loop repeats a block of code once for each item in a sequence [Source 1]. The most common use is looping over a range of numbers [Source 1]."*

The student reads this. Later they open their lecture notes, which say: *"A for loop iterates over any iterable — strings, lists, ranges, file lines, dictionary keys."*

The student's brain reads "any iterable" through the lens of "range of numbers." They underweight the broader definition because the chatbot's narrower framing came first. On an exam question about iterating over a list of strings, they pause — because their anchored understanding was range-based.

**The countermeasure:** when using the chatbot for a new topic, read the relevant notes first. Then use the chatbot to clarify specific parts. The anchor should be the notes — not the chatbot's summary.

---

### Confirmation Bias — Asking Questions That Confirm

The intro topic defined confirmation bias as seeking information that confirms what you already believe. In practice with the exam prep chatbot, it shows up in how students phrase questions.

**The confirmation-biased question:**
*"Can you confirm that a while loop is basically the same as a for loop just with a different condition?"*

**The neutral question:**
*"What is the difference between a while loop and a for loop?"*

The first question is phrased as a confirmation request. The chatbot, following the system prompt to answer from the notes, will search for content that supports the framing — and find partial support, because while loops and for loops do share some properties. The student receives partial confirmation of a subtly wrong understanding.

The second question invites the chatbot to surface the actual difference, which the notes cover directly.

**The countermeasure:** before asking the chatbot, write the question in neutral form. If you find yourself typing "confirm that" or "is it true that" — rewrite as "what does the note say about" or "what is the difference between."

### Availability Heuristic — Remembering the Impressive Moment

The intro topic defined the availability heuristic as judging reliability based on how easily an example comes to mind — usually the most vivid or recent one. This is the bias that was introduced there but not covered in depth. Here is how it specifically plays out.

**What this looks like:**

A student uses the chatbot for a week. Most answers are correct. On Tuesday, the chatbot gives an exceptional answer about a complex exception handling topic the student had been confused about for days. The answer is clear, well-structured, exactly what was needed at exactly the right moment. This becomes the student's mental reference point for "what this chatbot does."

On Thursday, the chatbot gives a subtly wrong answer about dictionary methods — confidently stating that `.update()` replaces the entire dictionary when it actually only updates or adds specified keys. The student's mind defaults to the vivid Tuesday memory. The chatbot was so impressively right that time. It must be right now too.

The availability heuristic causes the student to estimate reliability based on the most memorable experience — not the actual track record.

**What makes an experience vivid and memorable:**
- It resolved something confusing — high emotional relief
- It came at a time of high stress — the night before a test
- The answer was unusually long and detailed
- It saved a significant amount of time

None of these factors have anything to do with accuracy. They are features of the moment, not of the answer.

**The countermeasure:** track actual accuracy, not memorable moments. After each session, note which answers you verified and which turned out to be wrong. Over two weeks, you have a real track record — not a collection of vivid impressions. Trust the track record, not the memory.

## How Biases Get Encoded Into AI Systems

This is the dimension the intro topic did not cover. So far, the biases have all been on the human side — students using the chatbot in biased ways. But the chatbot itself was built by humans. Every step in that process was a place where bias could enter.

### Step 1 — Biased Training Data

AI language models are trained on text from the internet — articles, books, forum posts, documentation. That text was written by humans, and humans have biases. If the majority of programming tutorials explain for loops using range-based examples, the model learns that for loops and ranges are closely associated. A question about for loops is more likely to produce a range-based answer — even when the student is working with lists of strings.

**For the exam prep chatbot:** this matters less because the chatbot is constrained to the uploaded notes. But if the uploaded notes themselves reflect a bias — for example, if the lecturer always uses counting examples for loops and never iterating over strings — the chatbot will produce answers that reflect that bias. The chatbot did not introduce it. The source material did.

### Step 2 — Biased Labelling

When AI systems are trained with human feedback, humans label outputs as good or bad. Those humans have preferences, cultural assumptions, and blind spots. A labeller who finds formal academic language more trustworthy will rate formal answers higher — even when a simpler answer is more accurate for a student audience. Over thousands of labelling decisions, those preferences become embedded in what the model learns to produce.

**For the exam prep chatbot:** Claude was trained with human feedback. If the feedback preferred longer, more detailed answers, the chatbot may produce more detail than a student needs — which can overwhelm rather than clarify.

### Step 3 — Biased Evaluation

When an AI system is evaluated before deployment, the evaluators choose the test cases and decide what counts as a correct answer. If the evaluators never tested the system on edge cases — like questions at the exact boundary of the uploaded notes — those failures are invisible. The system passes evaluation and gets deployed with a blind spot the evaluation never found.

**For the exam prep chatbot:** the golden dataset evaluation covered this partially — testing with direct concept, comparison, out-of-scope, and edge case questions. But the golden dataset was designed by a human, and that human may have missed question types that a student would actually ask. The evaluation found what it was designed to find.

## The Four Biases — At a Glance

| Bias | What triggers it | What it causes | Countermeasure |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Automation bias** | Citation format looks like verification | Student skips clicking citations | Click every citation on revision-critical claims |
| **Anchoring** | Chatbot answer seen before reading notes | Notes interpreted through chatbot's frame | Read notes first, use chatbot to clarify |
| **Confirmation bias** | Question phrased as a confirmation request | Chatbot confirms a partial or wrong understanding | Rephrase as neutral: "what does the note say about X?" |
| **Availability heuristic** | One vivid accurate answer remembered | Reliability overestimated based on one moment | Track actual accuracy over time, not memorable moments |

## Best Practices

- Read the source notes before asking the chatbot about a new topic — this prevents the chatbot's framing from becoming the anchor
- Write questions in neutral form — "what does the note say about X" rather than "confirm that X is true"
- Click citations on any claim that will influence a revision decision — the citation format is not evidence of verification
- Track which chatbot answers turned out to be wrong over time — this gives you a real reliability track record instead of vivid impressions
- When the chatbot gives an unusually impressive answer, note it — but do not let it raise your general trust level

## Common Beginner Mistakes

- **Trusting a cited answer more than an uncited one** — both are generated by the same model. A citation tells you where to check, not that the claim is correct
- **Asking the chatbot first and notes second** — this sets the chatbot's framing as the anchor. The notes are the primary source; the chatbot is the clarification tool
- **Interpreting the chatbot's confidence as a signal of accuracy** — the model generates with consistent confidence regardless of whether the claim is grounded in the notes or drawn from general training
- **Assuming one impressive answer means all answers are reliable** — reliability is task-specific and question-specific, not a general property of the system

## Key Takeaways

- Automation bias shows up as trusting the `[Source N]` format as evidence of verification — without clicking the citation
- Anchoring shows up when the chatbot's answer seen first becomes the frame through which notes are read later
- Confirmation bias shows up in how questions are phrased — confirmation requests receive partial confirmation of wrong beliefs
- The availability heuristic shows up when one vivid accurate answer raises the general perceived reliability of the system
- Biases enter AI systems through training data, labelling decisions, and evaluation design — not just through how users interact with the output
- The countermeasure for each bias requires one deliberate habit change — clicking, sequencing, rephrasing, or tracking

> **Interview tip:** If asked "how do cognitive biases affect AI systems?" — describe both directions: how biases affect humans using AI outputs, and how biases get encoded into AI systems through training and labelling. Most candidates describe only one direction. Naming both — and giving a specific example of bias in training data — shows you understand AI systems as human artefacts, not neutral tools.
