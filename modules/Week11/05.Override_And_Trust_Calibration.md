## Overview

The Human-in-the-Loop Design topic established three models of human involvement and introduced the automation paradox — the finding that high AI reliability reduces human vigilance at exactly the moment vigilance is most needed. This topic addresses the decision that sits at the centre of every human-in-the-loop interaction: when should a human override the AI?

Override sounds simple. The AI says one thing, the human disagrees, the human overrides. But the decision is more complicated than it appears. Overriding when you should not wastes the AI's value and introduces human error where the AI was right. Not overriding when you should causes harm because the AI was wrong and the human knew it.

Getting this decision right consistently is what **trust calibration** means. Not trusting AI too much. Not trusting it too little. Trusting it proportionally to its demonstrated reliability on the specific task being asked of it.

---

## What an Override Is

An override is a human decision to reject an AI recommendation and substitute a human judgement instead.

Think of it like a GPS navigation system. The GPS says turn left. You are a local — you know that road is closed for construction today. You turn right instead. That is an override. You had information the GPS did not have. The override was correct.

Now imagine you are not a local. The GPS says turn left. You think the GPS might be wrong because you once took the wrong turn following a GPS in a different city. You turn right instead. The GPS was correct. You are now on the wrong road. That override was wrong — not because you overrode, but because your reason for overriding was not good enough.

The difference between a good override and a bad override is not whether you disagreed with the AI. It is whether you had a valid reason for disagreeing that the AI could not have accounted for.

---

## Four Situations Where Overriding Is Appropriate

### Situation 1 — You Have Information the AI Does Not

The AI's answer is based on what it was given. If you have relevant information that was not in the uploaded notes, not in the question, and not accessible to the system — and that information changes the correct answer — overriding is appropriate.

**In the exam prep chatbot:**
The chatbot answers a question about Python exception handling based on the uploaded notes. Your lecturer mentioned in last week's class — not in the notes — that this semester's exam will use a different exception handling convention. The chatbot cannot know this. Your override is correct: you supplement or replace the chatbot's answer with the information from class.

**The test:** would the AI have given a different answer if it had this information? If yes — override is appropriate. If no — the information is not relevant and the override is not justified by this reason.

---

### Situation 2 — The AI's Reasoning Contradicts a Known Fact

The AI produces an answer that contradicts something you know with high confidence to be true — from the notes, from class, from a verified source. Not something you think might be true. Something you can point to in a primary source.

**In the exam prep chatbot:**
The chatbot states that a `for` loop in Python requires a counter variable. You open the uploaded notes and read the exact sentence: "A `for` loop iterates over any iterable — it does not require a counter variable." The chatbot's claim contradicts the notes directly. Override: the notes are correct, the chatbot is wrong.

**The test:** can you point to a specific verified source that contradicts the AI's claim? If yes — override is appropriate. If you only feel the claim is wrong — that is not sufficient reason to override.

---

### Situation 3 — The AI is Operating Outside Its Reliable Range

Every AI system has a range of inputs it handles reliably and a range where it does not. When a question falls outside the reliable range — an edge case, an unusual combination, a topic the system was not designed for — the AI may still produce a confident-sounding answer that is unreliable.

**In the exam prep chatbot:**
The chatbot is reliable for direct concept questions from the uploaded Python notes. A student asks: "How does Python exception handling compare to Java exception handling?" This is a comparison question that requires knowledge the notes do not contain. The chatbot retrieves the closest Python content and generates an answer — but the Java comparison requires general training knowledge that may not be accurate. Override: the student should not act on this answer without verifying from a source that covers both languages.

**The test:** is this question within the system's demonstrated reliable range? If no — treat the answer as a starting point for further verification, not as a conclusion.

---

### Situation 4 — The Stakes Exceed the System's Reliability Threshold

Even when the AI is performing reliably on average, some individual decisions carry consequences high enough that the expected error rate is unacceptable. When the cost of a single wrong answer exceeds what is tolerable, override with full verification regardless of the system's general track record.

**In the exam prep chatbot:**
A student is revising for their final exam — 60% of their grade. The chatbot gives an answer about a topic that will definitely appear on the paper. Even if the chatbot is 95% reliable on direct concept questions, a 5% chance of acting on wrong information for a 60% exam paper is not acceptable. Override model: apply Model 1 — verify every citation before this answer goes into revision notes.

**The test:** what is the cost if this specific answer is wrong? If the cost is high enough that the expected error rate is unacceptable — override with full verification.

## Two Situations Where Overriding Is Not Appropriate

### Situation A — You Disagree But Cannot Explain Why

"It just feels wrong." "I think I remember it differently." "I'm not sure but something seems off."

These are signals to verify — not reasons to override. Feeling that an answer might be wrong is the starting point for investigation, not the conclusion. If investigation confirms the AI is wrong — override. If investigation confirms the AI is right — accept the answer, note that your instinct was incorrect, and update your calibration.

Overriding based on feeling without verification is substituting one unreliable source (the AI) for another unreliable source (unchecked human memory). Neither is verified. The verification step is what matters — not which direction you override in.

---

### Situation B — You Are Experiencing Confirmation Bias

You asked a question hoping for a particular answer. The AI gave a different answer. You want to override to get the answer you expected.

This is confirmation bias operating on the override decision itself. The question to ask is: did the AI give a different answer because it is wrong, or because your expected answer was wrong?

**The test:** before overriding, restate the question in a neutral form and ask it again. If the AI consistently gives the same answer across multiple neutral phrasings — your expected answer is likely the one that needs revising.

## What Trust Calibration Means

Trust calibration is the process of building and maintaining an accurate model of what an AI system can and cannot do reliably — and adjusting the level of scrutiny applied to its outputs proportionally.

**Miscalibrated trust has two failure modes:**

**Over-trust** — accepting AI outputs without appropriate scrutiny. The Air Canada customer over-trusted. The lawyer over-trusted. The physicians in the medical AI study over-trusted. Each paid a cost — financial, professional, clinical.

**Under-trust** — rejecting or not using AI outputs despite their reliability. A student who verifies every single chatbot answer — including routine syntax questions — wastes the efficiency gain that made the chatbot worth using. Worse, the verification effort creates fatigue, and fatigued verification is less reliable than calibrated spot-checking.

**Calibrated trust** is neither. It is proportional scrutiny — more checking where the system is less reliable, less checking where it is more reliable — based on a measured track record, not a feeling.

## Building a Personal Trust Calibration Record

Trust calibration requires evidence. Evidence requires tracking. This is the personal record a student should maintain over two weeks of using the exam prep chatbot.

**The calibration record — fill this in after each session:**

| Date | Question type | Question asked | Answer verified? | Verification result | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| | Direct concept | | Yes / No / Partial | Correct / Wrong / Partial | |
| | Comparison | | Yes / No / Partial | Correct / Wrong / Partial | |
| | Edge case | | Yes / No / Partial | Correct / Wrong / Partial | |
| | Out of scope | | Refused / Answered | Correct refusal / Wrong answer | |

**After two weeks — calculate the calibration scores:**

```text
Direct concept questions:    X correct out of Y verified  = __% reliable
Comparison questions:        X correct out of Y verified  = __% reliable
Edge case questions:         X correct out of Y verified  = __% reliable
Out of scope questions:      X correct refusals out of Y  = __% reliable
```

These four numbers are your calibration. They replace the feeling of "the chatbot seems reliable" with a measured track record per question type. The oversight model you apply to each question type should match its measured reliability — not your general impression of the system.

---

## Updating Calibration Over Time

Calibration is not a one-time measurement. It should be updated whenever:

- New files are uploaded — the knowledge base changed, reliability on some question types may change
- The Assistant instructions are modified — the system prompt affects how the model behaves
- A systematic error is found — one wrong answer in a specific category suggests the calibration for that category is optimistic
- A week has passed without any verification — the track record is stale and needs refreshing

**The recalibration trigger:**
If the weekly audit finds more than one wrong answer in five — that question type needs its oversight model raised by one level. Direct concept questions move from Model 3 to Model 2. Comparison questions move from Model 2 to Model 1.

If three consecutive weeks show zero errors in a question type — the oversight model can be lowered by one level, but never below Model 3. Periodic auditing is the minimum.

## Organisational Trust Calibration

Individual trust calibration applies to one student using one chatbot. Organisational trust calibration applies when an AI system is used by many people in an organisation — a company, a university, a hospital.

The principles are the same, but the mechanism is different. An organisation cannot rely on individual users to track their own calibration records. Instead, the organisation needs:

**Defined reliability thresholds** — before the system is deployed, the organisation specifies the minimum acceptable reliability for each task type. For example: "The chatbot must answer direct concept questions correctly at least 90% of the time before it is made available to students."

**Systematic measurement** — not individual users verifying their own answers, but a dedicated evaluation process running on a representative sample of outputs on a regular schedule.

**A response protocol** — when measured reliability falls below the threshold, a defined action is taken: the system is taken offline, the question type is flagged as unreliable, users are notified, the knowledge base is reviewed.

**Accountability assignment** — someone is responsible for monitoring the calibration and triggering the response protocol. Not the AI. Not the users. A named person or team.

For the exam prep chatbot deployed across a university course:
- The threshold might be: 85% correct on direct concept questions, 75% on comparison questions
- The measurement might be: weekly evaluation of 20 random answers per question type by a teaching assistant
- The response protocol might be: if reliability drops below threshold, a warning is added to the chatbot interface and the course team is notified
- The accountability: the module coordinator owns the calibration process

## Best Practices

- Build a calibration record from the first session — two weeks of tracked data is worth more than two months of impressions
- Set override thresholds before each session, not in the moment — "I will override if I can point to a source that contradicts this" is a rule; "I will override when it feels wrong" is not
- Never lower the oversight model below Model 3 — periodic auditing is the minimum regardless of how reliable the system appears
- When you override, write down why — the reason tells you which bias you were countering and whether the override was justified on reflection
- Treat under-trust as seriously as over-trust — refusing to use a reliable AI tool wastes time and creates unnecessary work

## Common Beginner Mistakes

- **Overriding on instinct without verification** — this substitutes one unverified source for another. Verify first, then decide whether to override
- **Treating high reliability as a reason to reduce oversight** — this is the automation paradox. Maintain the verification habit regardless of recent performance
- **Applying a single trust level to the whole system** — the chatbot may be 95% reliable on direct concept questions and 60% reliable on comparison questions. One number does not describe both
- **Never updating the calibration** — the knowledge base changes, the instructions change, question types change. A calibration from week one may not reflect week six

---

## Key Takeaways

- Override is appropriate when you have information the AI does not, when the AI contradicts a verified source, when the question is outside the reliable range, or when stakes exceed the acceptable error rate
- Override is not appropriate on feeling alone or to confirm a biased expectation — verification must come first
- Trust calibration is proportional scrutiny based on measured reliability per task type — not a general feeling about the system
- Both over-trust and under-trust are harmful — the goal is calibrated trust that matches scrutiny to demonstrated reliability
- Build a calibration record from the first session — four numbers (one per question type) replace impressions with evidence
- Organisational trust calibration requires defined thresholds, systematic measurement, a response protocol, and named accountability
- The automation paradox applies to calibration too — high reliability is the moment most likely to produce false confidence in the calibration itself

> **Interview tip:** If asked "how do you decide when to trust an AI system?" — describe trust calibration as a measurement process, not a feeling. Explain that you track accuracy per task type, calculate reliability scores, set oversight models based on those scores, and update when conditions change. Name both failure modes — over-trust and under-trust — and explain that the goal is calibrated scrutiny, not blanket trust or blanket scepticism. Most candidates say "I would test it first." Describing a tracked, per-task calibration process with defined update triggers shows you treat AI reliability as an empirical question with a measurable answer.
