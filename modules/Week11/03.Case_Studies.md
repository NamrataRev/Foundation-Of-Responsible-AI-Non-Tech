## Case Study 1 — The Air Canada Chatbot and Automation Bias

### What Happened

In 2024, a customer contacted Air Canada's website chatbot asking about the airline's bereavement fare policy — a discounted rate available to passengers travelling for a family member's funeral. The chatbot told the customer he could purchase a full-price ticket, travel, and then apply for the bereavement discount retroactively within 90 days.

This was wrong. Air Canada's policy required the bereavement fare to be applied before travel, not after. The customer followed the chatbot's instructions, paid full price, travelled, and then applied for the retroactive discount. Air Canada refused, citing the actual policy. The customer took Air Canada to the Civil Resolution Tribunal in British Columbia.

Air Canada argued in court that the chatbot was a "separate legal entity" responsible for its own statements. The tribunal rejected this argument and held Air Canada liable. The airline was ordered to pay the customer the difference between the full fare and the bereavement rate, plus interest and fees.

📎 [Air Canada chatbot ruling — CBC News](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/british-columbia/air-canada-chatbot-ruling-1.7116416)

### Which Bias Was Operating

**Automation bias.** The customer trusted the chatbot because it was an official airline system. An automated system on the airline's own website carries implied authority — it looks like it should know the airline's own policies. The customer did not independently check the published policy page. The system's appearance of authority substituted for verification.

The chatbot had no **refusal boundary for policy-critical questions**.

A correctly designed chatbot refuses consequential policy questions and directs the user to the verified source. Here is what correct refusal looks like in Pinecone:

![Pinecone playground showing correct refusal — chatbot says it cannot answer from the available notes and refuses to guess](../images/w10_02_retrieval_failure.png)

 When a customer asks about a financial policy with legal implications — bereavement fares, refund eligibility, compensation rights — the correct response is not to answer from training data. It is to direct the customer to the verified, current policy document or to a human agent.

A properly designed oversight mechanism would have looked like this:

```text
Customer: "Can I apply for the bereavement discount after I travel?"

Chatbot: "Bereavement fare policies can vary based on your booking type
and travel date. For decisions with financial implications, please check
our current policy at [link] or speak with an agent at [number]. I cannot
guarantee that policy details I provide are current."
```

This is a refusal — not a refusal to help, but a refusal to be the final authority on a consequential decision. The chatbot should have known the limits of its own reliability.

### The Lesson for the Exam Prep Chatbot

When the exam prep chatbot answers a question that will influence a revision decision — particularly an edge case or a topic the notes cover only briefly — the student needs to apply the same principle. The chatbot should not be the final authority on anything the student will act on. Check the notes. Check with the lecturer. The chatbot is a starting point, not a source of record.

## Case Study 2 — Legal Citation Hallucinations and Confirmation Bias

### What Happened

In 2023, a US lawyer named Steven Schwartz submitted a legal brief in a federal court case using citations generated by ChatGPT. The citations — case names, court details, and legal rulings — looked authentic. They were not. ChatGPT had fabricated them entirely: cases that did not exist, courts that had never issued the described rulings, citations formatted correctly but referring to nothing.

When opposing counsel could not locate the cited cases, the court ordered Schwartz to produce the original documents. He could not. The cases did not exist. The judge sanctioned Schwartz and his firm, finding that they had failed to verify the citations before submitting them to the court.

Schwartz later said he had not been aware that ChatGPT could generate false information. He had assumed it was like a search engine — finding real documents, not inventing them.

📎 [Lawyer's ChatGPT citations — The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2023/jun/23/two-us-lawyers-sanctioned-for-submitting-fake-chatgpt-court-citations)

### Which Bias Was Operating

**Confirmation bias combined with automation bias.** Schwartz had a legal argument he needed to support. He asked ChatGPT to find supporting cases. ChatGPT produced cases — with correct-looking formatting, court names, and citation styles — that appeared to support the argument. Schwartz read them as confirmation of his legal theory and did not ask whether they actually existed.

The confirmation bias led him to accept supporting evidence without scrutiny. The automation bias led him to trust the format — legal citation style — as evidence of authenticity.

### What Oversight Mechanism Was Missing

**Independent verification of every cited source.** In legal practice, every cited case must be verified against a primary legal database — Westlaw, LexisNexis, or equivalent — before it is submitted to a court. This is not optional. It is a professional obligation.

The oversight mechanism that was missing was not a technical one. It was a process step: verify the source exists before using it. The same step that every law student learns in their first year of legal research.

For AI-generated content, the equivalent process is: never treat a citation as real until you have confirmed the cited source exists and says what the AI claimed it says.

In Pinecone, clicking any citation shows the exact chunk — this is the verification step the lawyer skipped:

![Pinecone citation popup showing exact chunk text — clicking citations is the verification step that must happen before acting on any claim](../images/Verify_a_Citation.png)



### The Lesson for the Exam Prep Chatbot

When the exam prep chatbot cites `[Source 1]` for a claim, that citation means "this came from chunk 1 of the uploaded files." It does not mean the claim is correct. It does not mean the chunk actually says what the answer claimed. Click the citation. Read the chunk. Verify the claim appears in the chunk before acting on it. This is the legal research lesson applied to revision.

## Case Study 3 — Medical Over-Trust and the Availability Heuristic

### What Happened

A 2021 study published in the journal npj Digital Medicine examined how physicians interacted with an AI clinical decision support system that recommended medication dosages. The study found that when the AI system displayed a recommendation confidently — with no uncertainty indicator — physicians accepted the recommendation significantly more often, even in cases where the recommendation was incorrect.

More specifically: when the AI was wrong but displayed high confidence, physicians overrode the correct recommendation in favour of the AI's incorrect one. The AI's confident display of a recommendation created an anchoring effect — physicians adjusted their own clinical judgement toward the AI rather than maintaining their independent assessment.

The researchers noted that physicians who had previously seen the AI perform well in similar cases were more likely to over-trust it on the case where it failed — a direct instance of the availability heuristic.

📎 [Physician over-trust in AI — npj Digital Medicine](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41746-021-00503-7)

### Which Bias Was Operating

**Availability heuristic compounded by automation bias.** Physicians remembered cases where the AI had been right — vivid, recent, positive experiences. When the AI displayed confidence on a new case, those memories raised the apparent reliability of the current recommendation. The automation bias amplified this: a system designed by experts and integrated into a clinical workflow carries institutional authority.

### What Oversight Mechanism Was Missing

**Uncertainty display and mandatory independent check for high-stakes decisions.** The AI system displayed recommendations without communicating its confidence level or the range of cases where it performed reliably versus unreliably. A better-designed system would:

- Show a confidence level: "High confidence (based on 847 similar cases)" vs "Low confidence (fewer than 20 similar cases in training data)"
- Require explicit physician override documentation for high-stakes decisions
- Flag cases that fall outside the distribution the system was trained on

The oversight was missing at the design level — the system was not built to communicate its own uncertainty. Physicians had no signal to tell them when to apply more scrutiny.

### The Lesson for the Exam Prep Chatbot

Pinecone Assistant does not display a confidence level. Every answer looks equally authoritative — whether it is retrieving from a clear, relevant chunk or synthesising from several loosely related chunks. The student cannot tell from the answer format alone which is which.

This means the student must supply their own uncertainty signal. A useful rule of thumb:

| Question type | Apply this level of scrutiny |
|---|---|
| Direct concept from a clear heading in the notes | Spot-check one citation |
| Comparison requiring multiple chunks | Click all citations, verify each claim |
| Edge case at the boundary of the notes | Verify directly against the notes |
| Out of scope — should have been refused | Do not use the answer |

## What All Three Cases Share

Three different domains. Three different AI systems. Three different cognitive biases. But the same underlying failure pattern appears in all three:

**The human trusted the AI's output format as evidence of the output's correctness.**

- The Air Canada customer trusted the chatbot's authoritative tone.
- The lawyer trusted the legal citation format.
- The physicians trusted the confident recommendation display.

In each case, the format — not the content — was what created trust. And none of the formats contained any information about whether the underlying content was accurate.

This is why the countermeasures focus on process, not on the AI system itself. The format will always look convincing. The habit of verification is what catches the failures the format hides.

## Best Practices

- Treat every AI citation as a pointer to verify — not as proof that the source says what the AI claimed
- For decisions with consequences — financial, legal, medical, academic — never let the AI be the final authority
- Ask what the AI system cannot do before deciding how much to trust what it says it can do
- When a system displays high confidence, that is the moment to apply more scrutiny — not less

## Common Beginner Mistakes

- **Trusting format as evidence of accuracy** — legal citation style, footnote markers, formal tone, and confident phrasing all look correct regardless of whether the content is
- **Assuming official systems are verified** — a chatbot on a company's website has the same failure modes as any other chatbot. Official placement does not mean verified content
- **Treating past accuracy as a guarantee of current accuracy** — the AI that was right about while loops may be wrong about dictionary methods. Reliability is question-specific, not general

## Key Takeaways

- The Air Canada case shows automation bias producing legal liability — the customer trusted an official-looking system without verifying the policy directly
- The legal citations case shows confirmation bias combined with automation bias — the lawyer accepted AI-generated supporting evidence without checking whether the sources existed
- The medical over-trust case shows the availability heuristic compounded by automation bias — physicians remembered successful AI recommendations and extended that trust to a case where the AI was wrong
- All three cases share the same failure: the human trusted the output format as evidence of the output's correctness
- The missing oversight mechanism in each case was a process step — not a technical fix — that the human could have applied independently of the AI system
- For the exam prep chatbot: click citations, verify claims, and never let the chatbot be the final authority on anything that will influence a consequential decision

> **Interview tip:** If asked "give me a real example of AI oversight failure" — describe one of these three cases, name the bias, identify the missing oversight mechanism, and explain what the correct process should have been. Most candidates describe a vague "AI went wrong" scenario. Naming a specific case, a specific bias, and a specific missing process step shows you understand oversight as a design problem, not just a risk.
